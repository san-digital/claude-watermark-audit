For centuries the problem of finding one's longitude at sea remained one of the most stubborn puzzles in the history of navigation, a puzzle that cost thousands of sailors their lives and drove monarchs, scientists and inventors into a long and often bitter contest to solve it. Latitude, the position north or south of the equator, had been reasonably easy to determine since antiquity by measuring the angle of the sun at noon or the height of the pole star above the horizon. Longitude, the position east or west, offered no such simple celestial shortcut. A ship's navigator needed to know the local time at his current position and the time at a fixed reference point, usually a home port or an agreed prime meridian, at the very same moment; the difference between the two times, converted into degrees at the rate of fifteen degrees per hour, gave the longitude. Telling local time from the sun was straightforward. Knowing the time back home while hundreds or thousands of miles out to sea was the real difficulty, and no clock of the period could keep reliable time aboard a rolling, pitching, temperature-swinging ship for weeks or months on end.

By the early seventeenth century the stakes of this failure were already plain. Spanish and Portuguese sailors, followed by Dutch, French and English mariners, were crossing oceans in growing numbers, and dead reckoning, the practice of estimating position from course, speed and elapsed time, accumulated errors that grew worse the longer a voyage lasted. Ships ran aground on coasts they believed to be far away, missed islands they were aiming for, and sometimes vanished with no one ever learning quite where they had gone wrong. Philip II of Spain had offered a reward for a solution as early as 1567, and other European crowns followed with their own prizes and academies, drawing in the finest mathematicians and astronomers of the age, among them Galileo Galilei, who proposed using the eclipses of Jupiter's moons as a natural clock visible from anywhere on Earth. In principle the timings of these eclipses, tabulated in advance, could be compared with local observations to yield longitude; in practice the method demanded a telescope of considerable power and a steadiness of hand and platform that no rolling ship could offer, though it worked reasonably well on land and was used to help fix the longitude of continents and coastlines.

Two broad families of solution competed for the rest of the seventeenth and much of the eighteenth century. The first was astronomical, relying on the predictable motions of the heavens to serve as a clock in the sky. The lunar distance method, refined over decades by astronomers including Tobias Mayer and Nevil Maskelyne, measured the angle between the moon and a chosen star or the sun, an angle that changes steadily as the moon moves against the background of fixed stars. With sufficiently accurate tables predicting these angles for a reference meridian, usually Greenwich once the method became standard in Britain, a navigator could work out the time at that reference point purely from observation, then compare it with local time to compute longitude. The method required no mechanical clock at all, only a sextant and, unfortunately, several hours of laborious spherical trigonometry for every fix, work that was slow, exhausting and prone to error when performed by a tired officer on a moving deck.

The second family of solution was mechanical: build a clock robust enough to keep home time accurately for the whole of a long voyage. This was widely regarded by the greatest scientific minds of the era, including Isaac Newton, as effectively impossible, since existing pendulum clocks depended on a steady, level platform and were sensitive to temperature, humidity and the corrosive effects of salt air, none of which a ship at sea could offer. The urgency of the problem was thrown into sharp relief by disaster. In 1707 a squadron of British warships under Sir Cloudesley Shovell, badly misjudging its position, ran onto the rocks of the Isles of Scilly in poor visibility, and some two thousand sailors drowned. Public and parliamentary pressure that followed led to the Longitude Act of 1714, which established a Board of Longitude and offered a substantial cash prize, scaled to the accuracy achieved, for any method that could determine a ship's longitude to within specified tolerances on a voyage to the West Indies.

It was against this backdrop that a self-taught Yorkshire carpenter and clockmaker named John Harrison set out to prove the sceptics wrong. Beginning in the 1730s, Harrison built a succession of increasingly refined marine timekeepers, known today as H1 through H4, each one attacking the problems of temperature compensation, friction and the need for oil-free, self-correcting mechanisms with fresh ingenuity. His early machines, H1 through H3, were large, heavy, and full of inventive but ultimately impractical solutions such as counterbalanced beams intended to cancel out the ship's motion. His breakthrough came with H4, completed in 1759, a large but portable watch roughly five inches across, quite unlike its bulky predecessors, which used a fast-beating balance wheel rather than a pendulum and included a novel temperature-compensated balance spring. On a trial voyage to Jamaica in 1761 and 1762, H4 lost only a handful of seconds over more than two months at sea, an astonishing feat of precision that comfortably exceeded the standard required for the top prize.

Harrison's triumph did not translate smoothly into reward. The Board of Longitude, dominated by astronomers with a professional and financial interest in the lunar distance method, subjected him to years of delay, additional trials and shifting demands before he received the bulk of the promised money, and even then only after direct intervention by King George III late in Harrison's life. Meanwhile the watchmaker Larcum Kendall was commissioned to produce a copy of H4, known as K1, which Captain James Cook carried on his second voyage of exploration and praised for its remarkable reliability. Other makers, notably John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw in Britain, went on to simplify and standardise Harrison's ideas, developing designs that could be produced in greater numbers at lower cost, using detent escapements that became the standard mechanism for marine chronometers well into the twentieth century. By the early nineteenth century chronometers had become standard equipment on naval and merchant vessels of the major seafaring powers, carried in pairs or threes so that navigators could cross-check readings and identify a chronometer that had begun to drift. The lunar distance method persisted for some decades afterward as a valuable check and a fallback for vessels without a reliable chronometer, but it steadily lost ground as chronometers became cheaper, sturdier and more widely trusted. The eventual adoption of Greenwich as the international prime meridian in 1884 owed much to the dominance of British chronometers and charts built around Greenwich time, cementing in international convention a solution that had begun a century and a half earlier with one stubborn craftsman's refusal to accept that a clock could never survive the sea.
