French cuisine is often discussed in terms of its regions rather than as a single tradition, and nowhere is that more obvious than in the contrast between the rich, butter-and-cream cooking of the north and the olive-oil-driven cuisine of the Mediterranean south. In Normandy and Brittany, dishes lean on dairy, cider, and seafood, while in Provence, tomatoes, garlic, and herbes de Provence dominate dishes like ratatouille and the fish stew bouillabaisse, a Marseille speciality traditionally made with several kinds of Mediterranean fish and a saffron-scented broth, served with a garlicky rouille sauce spread on toasted bread. Further inland, the Périgord region of southwestern France is famous for foie gras and black truffles, ingredients that appear in everything from simple pan-seared preparations to elaborate terrines, while nearby Bordeaux lends its name to wine-based sauces built on reductions of the region's own red wine.

Bourgogne, or Burgundy, gave French cooking two of its most enduring dishes: boeuf bourguignon, beef slowly braised in red wine with mushrooms and pearl onions, and coq au vin, a similar treatment applied to chicken, both dishes born from the practical need to tenderize tougher cuts of meat over long, slow cooking. Alsace, on the German border, brings a different sensibility entirely, favouring choucroute garnie, a hearty dish of sauerkraut with sausages and cured pork, alongside crisp white wines like Riesling and Gewürztraminer. And no survey of French food would be complete without its desserts: crème brûlée, with its shattering caramelized sugar crust over silky custard, crème caramel, tarte Tatin, the upside-down caramelized apple tart said to have been invented by accident at a hotel in the Sologne, and the endless variety of pâtisserie found in nearly every French town, from mille-feuille to éclairs to the humble but perfect croissant.

Spanish cuisine shares some Mediterranean instincts with southern France but develops them very differently, shaped heavily by the country's Moorish history, its long Atlantic and Mediterranean coastlines, and strong regional identities. In Andalucía, in the deep south, gazpacho, a chilled soup of tomatoes, peppers, cucumber, and bread, offers relief from summer heat, while fried fish, pescaíto frito, reflects the region's coastal abundance. Jamón ibérico, cured ham from black Iberian pigs that forage on acorns in the dehesa woodlands, is treated with an almost ceremonial reverence, sliced thin by hand and served simply, often alongside manchego cheese and a glass of sherry from Jerez. Moving north and east into Cataluña, the cooking becomes more experimental, home to pan con tomate, bread rubbed with ripe tomato and olive oil, and to the roots of modern culinary techniques championed by chefs associated with the region's avant-garde restaurant scene.

The Basque Country, straddling the border with France, has its own distinct food culture built around pintxos, small snacks pinned with toothpicks onto slices of bread and served in bars across San Sebastián, alongside a strong tradition of seafood cookery and the lightly sparkling white wine txakoli. Paella, perhaps Spain's most internationally recognized dish, actually originates specifically from Valencia, traditionally made with rabbit, chicken, and green beans cooked with rice and saffron in a wide, shallow pan, though seafood versions have become far more common outside the region itself. Tortilla española, a thick omelette of eggs, potatoes, and onion, appears everywhere from tapas bars to home kitchens, a dish simple enough to make daily yet debated endlessly over whether onion belongs in it at all.

Portuguese cuisine, though often overshadowed internationally by its Spanish neighbour, has its own strong identity built around the Atlantic. Bacalhau, dried and salted cod, is so central to Portuguese cooking that it is said there are as many recipes for it as there are days in the year, prepared boiled, baked, fried, or shredded into dishes like bacalhau à brás, mixed with matchstick potatoes, eggs, and olives. Along the Algarve coast in the south, cataplana, a dish of clams, sausage, and vegetables cooked in a distinctive hinged copper pan of the same name, reflects the strong Moorish and Mediterranean influence on Portuguese coastal cooking. In the north, around Porto, the francesinha, a towering sandwich of cured meats and sausage smothered in melted cheese and a spiced tomato and beer sauce, represents a heartier, more indulgent tradition, often paired with the region's famous fortified wine or, more casually, a glass of the young, slightly effervescent vinho verde produced in the Minho region.

Portugal's sweets lean heavily on eggs and sugar, a legacy of convents that once produced pastries to use up egg yolks left over after using egg whites to starch clothing and clarify wine. The most famous result is the pastel de nata, a small custard tart with a flaky, blistered pastry shell, best eaten warm with a dusting of cinnamon, and closely associated with the Belém district of Lisbon, where the original recipe is still guarded as a closely held secret. Piri-piri, a fiery sauce made from small African bird's eye chillies brought back through Portugal's historic maritime trade routes, seasons grilled chicken across the country and has since spread internationally through Portuguese-influenced restaurants.

Taken together, these three cuisines show how geography, history, and trade shaped what ended up on the table: French cooking refined by regional wine and dairy traditions, Spanish cooking layered with Moorish spice routes and coastal abundance, and Portuguese cooking defined by its long relationship with the Atlantic Ocean and the trade networks it once commanded. Despite sharing a peninsula and, in places, a border, Spanish and Portuguese food remain distinctly their own, just as Provençal cooking bears little resemblance to the richer fare of Alsace or Bourgogne further north, a reminder that national cuisines are rarely single things at all.
