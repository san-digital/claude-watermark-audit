The waiting room smelled of wet wool and old radiators. Frances sat closest to the fire, her suitcase balanced on her knees like a shield, while Tomas paced by the window, checking his watch every few minutes as though that might hurry the train along.

"You'll wear a groove in the floor," said the third occupant, an elderly man named Griggs who'd claimed the bench by the door an hour earlier and hadn't moved since. "Sit down. It's not coming any faster for your pacing."

Tomas didn't sit. "My sister's expecting me at six. If I miss this one, I don't get another until—" He stopped mid-sentence, glancing at the departures board as if it might have changed in the last thirty seconds.

"Until tomorrow," Frances finished for him, not unkindly. "We know. You've said it four times."

Griggs chuckled, a dry sound like paper rustling. "Reminds me of my own father. Man never sat still in a waiting room in his life. Used to say, 'A watched train's like a watched pot, it takes its sweet time out of spite.'" He shook his head. "Course, he also missed his own brother's wedding by pacing straight past the platform, so take that wisdom as you like."

Frances laughed despite herself, and even Tomas's shoulders eased a fraction. "That's oddly comforting," he admitted, finally lowering himself onto the bench beside her (though he kept his coat buttoned, ready to bolt the moment the tannoy crackled).

"It wasn't meant to be comforting," Griggs said. "It was meant to be true. There's a difference, though not always a large one."

A distant rumble rolled through the walls, and all three of them straightened at once, Tomas's suitcase already half off his lap.

"That'll be it," Frances said, standing.

"Or the 4:15 to somewhere else entirely," Griggs muttered, not moving from his bench in the slightest. "I've learned not to trust the first sound. Wait for the second one, the one that actually stops."

They waited. The second rumble came, closer this time, and the tannoy finally crackled to life overhead. Tomas was already at the door before the announcement finished, calling back over his shoulder, "Thank you, both of you, really," in a voice that cracked slightly with relief.

Griggs didn't look up from his newspaper. "Go on then. Don't miss your sister's dinner on my account."

Frances lingered a moment longer, watching the old man settle back into his corner of the room as though he intended to wait there forever.
