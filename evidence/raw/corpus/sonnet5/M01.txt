The Panama Canal's system of locks was engineered to lift ships over the isthmus's central highlands rather than attempt an impossibly deep sea-level cut through solid rock. When the canal opened in 1914, after French efforts under Ferdinand de Lesseps had collapsed in the 1880s amid disease and financial ruin, American engineers led by John Stevens and George Goethals adopted a lock-based design instead. Ships travelling from the Atlantic pass through the three-step Gatun Locks, which raise them 26 metres to the level of Gatun Lake, an artificial reservoir created by damming the Chagres River. Vessels then cross the lake and the Culebra Cut, a channel blasted through the continental divide, before descending through the Pedro Miguel and Miraflores Locks on the Pacific side.

Each lock chamber works on a simple principle of gravity-fed water transfer. Enormous valves open to let water flow from a higher chamber, or from the adjoining lake, into a lower one, raising or lowering the ship enclosed within massive concrete walls. No pumps are required for this movement, since the entire system relies on gravity alone; only the machinery that opens and closes the miter gates and valves needs power. Electric locomotives, known as mules, run on tracks along the lock walls and use cables to keep ships centred as they pass through the narrow chambers, since the vessels move under their own power but cannot steer precisely enough in such tight confines.

For nearly a century the original locks, each 33.5 metres wide, effectively capped the size of ship that could use the canal, a limit shipbuilders came to call Panamax. Growing global trade and ever larger container ships eventually made this constraint a serious bottleneck. A major expansion project, completed in 2016, added a third, wider lane of locks alongside the originals. These new locks use rolling gates instead of miter gates and incorporate water-saving basins that reuse roughly sixty percent of the water in each transit, reducing the enormous draw on Gatun Lake's freshwater supply. The expanded canal now accommodates Neopanamax vessels carrying far more cargo than the original locks ever could, keeping this hundred-year-old feat of engineering central to world shipping.
