When the Panama Canal opened to traffic in August 1914, its solution to crossing the isthmus was not to dig a sea-level channel straight through the mountainous spine of Panama, an approach the French had already tried and abandoned at enormous human cost in the 1880s, but to lift ships bodily over the high ground using a staircase of locks. The American engineering team that took over the project in 1904, led first by John Stevens and later by George Goethals, judged a lock canal far more achievable than the sea-level design de Lesseps had favoured, and also far more resistant to the landslides that had plagued excavation work in the Culebra Cut.

Ships entering from the Caribbean side climb through the three chambers of the Gatun Locks, rising a combined 26 metres until they reach Gatun Lake, a vast reservoir formed by damming the Chagres River specifically to supply the canal with water and to shorten the distance vessels needed to travel under lock power alone. After crossing the lake and threading through the Culebra Cut, ships descend again through the Pedro Miguel Lock and then the two-stage Miraflores Locks before reaching the Pacific.

The locks themselves move ships using nothing but gravity. Water flows downhill from the lake through large culverts into a lock chamber, raising the vessel inside to match the level of the next chamber up, or drains out again to lower it, all without a single pump. Massive steel miter gates, some of the largest moving structures ever built when the canal opened, seal each chamber, while small electric locomotives called mules run along tracks on the lock walls, using cables to keep the ship centred since it cannot manoeuvre itself precisely in such a narrow space.

For most of the canal's history, the width of these original chambers, just over 33 metres, effectively defined the maximum size of oceangoing vessel, a category shipbuilders still refer to as Panamax. As container ships grew far beyond that limit through the late twentieth century, Panama undertook a second major construction effort, completing a new, wider set of locks in 2016 that use sliding rather than miter gates and recycle a large share of their water through adjacent basins. A century after its first transit, the canal remains one of the few pieces of infrastructure whose basic engineering principle, water moving ships uphill and down by gravity alone, has changed remarkably little.
