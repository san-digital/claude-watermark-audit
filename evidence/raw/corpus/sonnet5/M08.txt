Vienna and Prague sit at the heart of two overlapping musical traditions, one Austro-German and one Czech, that shaped much of what we now call classical music. Vienna's claim is the more famous: Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, and later Brahms all worked in the city, drawn by imperial patronage and a dense concentration of aristocratic households eager to fund private orchestras and chamber ensembles. The Vienna Philharmonic, founded in 1842, still performs in the Musikverein's gilded Golden Hall, an auditorium whose acoustics are frequently cited as among the finest in the world. Later, Vienna became the unlikely birthplace of a very different tradition when Arnold Schönberg developed twelve-tone technique in the early twentieth century, abandoning conventional tonality altogether and influencing generations of composers who followed his lead, including his students Alban Berg and Anton Webern.

Prague's musical identity grew more explicitly nationalist. Bedřich Smetana, often considered the father of Czech classical music, composed his cycle of symphonic poems Má vlast as a tribute to the Bohemian landscape and history, with its famous movement Vltava tracing the course of the river through the countryside and into Prague itself. Antonín Dvořák, who studied under Smetana's influence, brought Czech folk idioms into symphonic and chamber forms with enormous international success, later spending several years in the United States, where he drew on African American spirituals and Native American melodies for his New World Symphony before returning home. Leoš Janáček took Czech nationalism somewhere stranger and more modern, building his operatic style directly on the rhythms and inflections of spoken Czech speech, producing works like Jenůfa and The Cunning Little Vixen that sound like almost nothing else from the period.

The two cities were never entirely separate. Mozart considered Prague his most appreciative audience and premiered Don Giovanni there in 1787 rather than in Vienna, reportedly telling friends that Praguers understood him better than his own countrymen did. French composers occasionally entered this Central European conversation too; Gabriel Fauré never lived in either city, but his harmonic language influenced how later Czech and Austrian composers thought about colour and ambiguity within otherwise traditional forms. Together, Vienna's institutional grandeur and Prague's folk-rooted intensity produced two of the richest and most distinct classical music cultures in Europe, still audible in concert halls and opera houses across both cities today.
