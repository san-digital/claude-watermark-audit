1. Give yourself sixteen to twenty weeks. Half marathon fitness is built, not conjured, and rushing a plan into eight or ten weeks is the single most common reason first-timers end up injured or exhausted on race day. A longer runway lets you build volume gradually, absorb a bad week without panicking, and still arrive undertrained rather than overtrained, which is the safer place to be.

2. Follow the ten percent rule for mileage. Increase your total weekly distance by roughly ten percent at most from one week to the next, and build in a lighter step-back week every third or fourth week. Your cardiovascular system adapts faster than your tendons, ligaments and bones, and it is almost always the connective tissue that fails first when mileage climbs too quickly.

3. Get fitted for proper running shoes. Visit a specialist running shop and get your gait assessed rather than guessing your size and picking whatever looks fastest on the shelf. The right shoe for your foot shape and stride can be the difference between finishing comfortably and nursing blisters or shin pain for the last five miles.

4. Make your long run the anchor of the week. One run, growing steadily longer over the weeks, teaches your body to burn fat efficiently, strengthens the specific muscles used in sustained running, and builds the mental resilience of simply being on your feet for two hours or more. Everything else in the plan supports this one session.

5. Respect your easy days. It feels unproductive to run slowly, but easy runs done at a genuinely conversational pace let you recover, build aerobic capacity, and arrive at your harder sessions and long runs actually able to do them justice. Most new runners run their easy days too hard and their hard days too easy, which flattens all the benefit out of both.

6. Practise your fuelling strategy in training, never on race day. Try different gels, chews or sports drinks on your long runs so you know exactly what your stomach tolerates and roughly how often you need to take something in, typically every thirty to forty-five minutes once you're running past an hour. Race day is not the moment to discover that a particular gel gives you cramps.

7. Add strength training twice a week. Short sessions focused on your glutes, hips, core and calves build the stability that keeps your running form intact when you're tired in the closing miles. You don't need a gym membership or heavy weights; bodyweight squats, lunges, planks and calf raises done consistently make a real difference.

8. Learn to run by effort, not just pace. GPS watches are useful but wind, hills, heat and fatigue all change what a given pace actually costs you on a given day. Training yourself to recognise easy, moderate and hard effort by breathing and perceived exertion means you can adjust sensibly when conditions on race day don't match your spreadsheet.

9. Rehearse race-day logistics in advance. Know exactly how you'll get to the start, where to park or which train to catch, when bag drop closes, and how long the queue for the toilets is likely to be. A stressful, rushed morning burns nervous energy you'll want later, so remove as many unknowns as you can before you even start running.

10. Don't wear anything new on race day. New shoes, new socks, a new shirt with a seam you've never tested for twelve miles, all of it is a gamble. Wear kit you've already run long distances in, and if you want new gear for the race, break it in on a training run at least two weeks beforehand.

11. Include some hill work, even on a flat course. Hill repeats build leg strength and running economy that transfer directly to flat, fast running, and they raise your heart rate without the joint impact of sprinting. Even a hilly race course becomes far less intimidating once your legs already know what sustained climbing feels like.

12. Practise negative splits on your long runs. Aim to run the second half of a long training run slightly faster than the first, which trains both the physical discipline of holding back early and the mental habit of finishing strong. Runners who go out too fast in the first three miles of a half marathon are the ones who suffer most in the last three.

13. Taper properly in the final two weeks. Cut your mileage down significantly while keeping a little intensity in short bursts, so your legs arrive at the start line rested rather than fatigued from a training block you're still recovering from. Resist the urge to squeeze in one more big session out of nerves; it will cost you more than it gives you.

14. Sort out your hydration plan before race week. Know whether the race provides water or sports drink at aid stations, and practise drinking from a cup while running if you've never done it, because it's a genuinely fiddly skill. Dehydration and overhydration are both real risks, so aim to drink to thirst rather than forcing down more than you need.

15. Deal with niggles immediately, not eventually. A tight calf or a sore knee that you run through for three more weeks because you don't want to lose training days usually turns into the injury that actually stops you racing. A few days of rest, some gentle stretching or a visit to a physio early on is far cheaper than the alternative.

16. Prioritise sleep during your training block. Your body does most of its adaptation and repair while you're asleep, not while you're running, and consistently short sleep undermines recovery, immune function and motivation all at once. Treat a good night's sleep before your key long runs with the same seriousness you'd give the run itself.

17. Break the race into mental chunks. Thirteen point one miles is a long way to hold in your head as a single block, so plan to think of it in stages, the first few miles settling in, the middle miles finding rhythm, and the last three or four as their own separate effort where you dig in. This makes the distance feel manageable rather than overwhelming.

18. Know your pacing plan and write it down. Decide roughly what pace per mile gets you to your goal, and consider writing the splits on your arm or wrist so you're not doing maths at mile ten when you're tired. Starting even ten or fifteen seconds per mile too fast in the excitement of the start line routinely costs far more time later.

19. Find a training partner or join a club. Running with other people, even just for one session a week, makes hard workouts feel easier, keeps you accountable on the mornings you'd rather stay in bed, and gives you somewhere to ask the questions that come up constantly when you're new to this. Shared miles also tend to be genuinely more enjoyable miles.

20. Study the course profile before race day. Know where the hills are, where the tight turns are, and roughly where you'll hit the halfway point, so nothing on the route catches you by surprise. A hill you knew was coming at mile nine feels very different to a hill you discover on your legs with no warning.

21. Expect a wall somewhere between mile nine and eleven. Even well-trained first-timers often hit a rough patch in the last quarter of the race where motivation and energy both dip sharply. Have a plan for it in advance, whether that's a mantra, breaking the remaining distance into smaller goals, or simply expecting the low patch to pass, because it usually does within a mile or two.

22. Set a realistic first-time goal, or none at all. There is real value in racing your first half marathon purely to finish strong and enjoy it, saving the serious time-chasing for your second attempt once you know what the distance actually demands of you. A goal built from your training paces rather than a number you'd simply like to see is far more likely to leave you satisfied.

23. Warm up properly before the start. A few minutes of easy jogging and some dynamic stretches or leg swings in the corral get your muscles ready and settle pre-race nerves, which matters more over thirteen miles than it might for a shorter race. Standing still and cold for forty minutes before the gun goes is not the ideal preparation it might feel like.

24. Plan your post-race recovery before you need it. Have warm clothes, food and something to drink ready for the moment you cross the line, because your body cools down fast once you stop moving and the immediate aftermath is a poor time to be making decisions. A short walk, some gentle stretching and a proper meal within a couple of hours all help you recover faster than collapsing on the sofa the second you get home.

25. Remember that finishing is the achievement. It's easy, surrounded by other people's times and social media splits, to lose sight of the fact that simply training consistently for months and completing thirteen point one miles under your own power is a genuinely significant thing to have done. Whatever the clock says when you cross that line, treat it as the first data point in a longer running story, not a verdict on the whole effort.
