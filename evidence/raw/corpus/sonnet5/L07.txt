The London Underground began not as a single, planned system but as a set of competing private railway schemes built to relieve appalling road congestion in the world's largest city during the mid nineteenth century. The first section, the Metropolitan Railway, opened on 10 January 1863 between Paddington and Farringdon Street. It was built using the cut and cover method, in which a trench was dug along an existing road, brick tunnels were built inside it, and the road was then relaid on top. Steam locomotives hauled the original trains, and although this filled the tunnels with smoke and steam, ventilation shafts and open sections helped clear the air enough that the service proved immediately popular, carrying nearly 40,000 passengers on its opening day alone.

Success bred rapid expansion. The Metropolitan Railway extended west and north through the 1860s and 1870s, eventually reaching as far as Amersham and Chesham in the county of Buckinghamshire, a stretch that later gave rise to the term Metro-land, used to describe the new suburbs built along the line. A rival company, the Metropolitan District Railway, opened in 1868 and worked with the Metropolitan to complete an inner ring of track around central London, finished in 1884 and known today as the Circle line. Both companies used similar cut and cover construction and both suffered from smoke-filled stations, since steam engines remained the only practical form of traction until electric power became reliable enough for underground use.

A very different kind of underground railway appeared in 1890, when the City and South London Railway opened between King William Street in the City and Stockwell south of the river. Rather than digging a trench from the surface, engineers used a circular tunneling shield, a technique refined by Peter William Barlow and James Henry Greathead, to bore a narrow tube deep below the streets, avoiding the need to disturb buildings, sewers, and existing infrastructure above. Because steam locomotives could not operate safely in such confined tunnels, the line used electric traction from the outset, making it the world's first major electric underground railway and giving the network its enduring nickname, the Tube.

The years around 1900 brought a burst of new deep tube lines, financed in large part by the American transport magnate Charles Tyson Yerkes, who arrived in London after a controversial career building streetcar and elevated railway systems in Chicago. Yerkes consolidated several struggling and half-built tube schemes into the Underground Electric Railways Company of London, which opened the Bakerloo, Piccadilly, and part of what became the Northern line in quick succession between 1906 and 1907. His companies also electrified the existing District Railway and introduced a shared architectural style for stations, most visibly the distinctive ox-blood glazed tiles designed by the architect Leslie Green, many of which survive on station facades to this day.

Yerkes died in 1906, before most of his projects were finished, but the organisation he built survived and grew into the London Passenger Transport Board, formed in 1933 to unify the Underground, buses, and trams under a single public authority. This period is closely associated with Frank Pick, the managing director whose insistence on consistent design gave the network its familiar visual identity, including the red and blue roundel symbol and the modernist Johnston typeface still used across the system. In 1933 the Underground also adopted a radically simplified diagrammatic map designed by an electrical draughtsman named Harry Beck, who treated the network more like a circuit diagram than a geographically accurate map, spacing stations evenly and using only horizontal, vertical, and 45 degree lines. Passengers loved it immediately, and variations of Beck's design are still used by transit systems around the world.

During the Second World War many stations took on an unplanned second role as air raid shelters, with Londoners sleeping on platforms and in disused tunnels through the Blitz, sometimes in huge numbers organised with bunks, canteens, and even makeshift libraries. Some unfinished or little used sections were converted into aircraft factories or government offices, hidden from view beneath the ordinary business of running trains above them. After the war, several deep level shelters built specifically for wartime use were later opened to the public or repurposed for storage and telecommunications.

The decades after the war brought a period of relative stagnation, as investment favoured roads and the newly nationalised railways elsewhere, and only two entirely new lines were built in the second half of the twentieth century. The Victoria line, opened in stages between 1968 and 1971, was the first line built right across central London since the 1900s and introduced automatic train operation, in which trains ran under computer control with a driver present mainly to open and close doors and respond to emergencies. The Jubilee line followed in 1979, initially reusing existing tunnels before a major extension opened in 1999 to serve the redeveloped docklands area around Canary Wharf, built with unusually large stations designed to handle heavy future demand.

The twenty-first century Underground has focused less on building new lines and more on modernising what already exists, replacing worn out signalling, refurbishing stations, and extending step free access to a network that was, for the most part, designed and built more than a century before accessibility became a legal requirement. The most significant recent addition to London's rail network, the Elizabeth line, opened in 2022, and though technically operated separately from the Underground, it shares stations and ticketing with the older system and was built using the same tunneling expertise developed over more than a hundred and fifty years. Today the Underground carries around five million passenger journeys on an average weekday across eleven lines and more than two hundred and fifty stations, remaining both a practical necessity for the city and a piece of engineering history still visible in its Victorian brickwork, its ox-blood tiles, and the deceptively simple map that Harry Beck sketched out in his spare time nearly a century ago.
