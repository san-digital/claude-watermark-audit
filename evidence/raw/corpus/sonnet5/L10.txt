For most of the age of sail, navigators could determine their latitude, their position north or south of the equator, with reasonable confidence by measuring the angle of the sun at noon or of the pole star at night, tools and calculations well understood since antiquity. Longitude, position east or west, offered no such straightforward solution, and this gap between what sailors could and could not measure caused shipwrecks, lost cargo, and thousands of deaths throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as vessels missed their intended landfalls or ran aground on coastlines they believed to be safely distant.

The underlying problem was fundamentally one of time rather than geometry. Because the earth rotates a full 360 degrees in 24 hours, each hour of difference between local time aboard ship and the time at a known reference point, such as the home port, corresponds to exactly 15 degrees of longitude. A navigator who could compare the ship's local time, easily found by observing when the sun reached its highest point, against the time back home would be able to calculate exactly how far east or west the ship had travelled. The difficulty was keeping accurate time at home while at sea, since no clock of the period could survive the temperature swings, humidity, and constant motion of an ocean voyage without drifting wildly off true time within days.

Several European governments recognised the severity of the problem and offered substantial prizes for a workable solution. Spain and the Netherlands made early attempts at incentivising research in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, but the most consequential prize came from the British government, which passed the Longitude Act in 1714 following a series of naval disasters, most notoriously the wrecking of four ships of Sir Cloudesley Shovell's fleet on the Isles of Scilly in 1707, an incident that killed close to two thousand sailors and was widely, if not entirely fairly, blamed on a failure to determine position accurately. The Act established a Board of Longitude and offered a reward of up to twenty thousand pounds, an enormous sum at the time, for any method capable of determining longitude at sea to within specified degrees of accuracy.

Two broad approaches competed for the prize over the following decades. The first, favoured by most astronomers of the period, relied on observing predictable celestial events, most importantly the position of the moon relative to the stars, a method known as the lunar distance technique. By comparing the observed position of the moon against tables calculated in advance for a known location, typically the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, a skilled navigator could work out the time at that reference point and therefore the ship's longitude. The method worked, but it demanded difficult sextant observations, lengthy calculations that could take hours to complete by hand, and clear skies, none of which were reliably available aboard a rolling ship in bad weather. Nevil Maskelyne, who became Astronomer Royal in 1765, championed this approach and published the Nautical Almanac from 1767 onward, providing pre-calculated lunar distance tables that made the method far more practical for ordinary navigators to use.

The second approach, championed almost single-handedly by a self-taught Yorkshire carpenter and clockmaker named John Harrison, aimed to solve the problem mechanically rather than astronomically: build a clock accurate and robust enough to keep the home port's time reliably throughout a long ocean voyage, regardless of temperature changes, humidity, or the ship's motion. Harrison began work on the problem in the 1730s and spent the better part of four decades refining a series of increasingly sophisticated timekeepers. His first attempt, H1, completed in 1735, was a large and heavy machine built from wood and brass that used counterbalanced weights to compensate for the ship's rolling motion, and it performed well enough on a sea trial to encourage further funding from the Board of Longitude. Harrison continued refining his designs through H2 and H3, each addressing weaknesses discovered in the previous version, before making a crucial conceptual shift toward a much smaller and more portable design.

His fourth attempt, H4, completed in 1759, looked almost nothing like its predecessors: rather than a large mechanism, it resembled an oversized pocket watch, only about thirteen centimetres across, using a fast-beating balance wheel and a novel temperature-compensation mechanism to maintain accuracy despite changing conditions. On a trial voyage to Jamaica in 1761 and 1762, H4 lost only about five seconds over an eighty-one day voyage, an extraordinary result that comfortably exceeded the accuracy required for the full prize. Despite this success, the Board of Longitude, dominated by astronomers who favoured the lunar distance method and were reluctant to accept that a single craftsman working largely alone had solved a problem that had defeated the finest scientific minds in Europe, resisted awarding Harrison the full prize for years, demanding repeated trials, duplicate copies of his mechanism, and eventually direct intervention from King George III before Harrison received a substantial payment in 1773, though he was never formally awarded the prize outright.

Harrison's design proved that a marine chronometer was not only possible but practical, and once his methods became known, other clockmakers, particularly Larcum Kendall, who built a close copy known as K1, refined and eventually mass-produced chronometers based on similar principles. Captain James Cook carried a Kendall chronometer on his second voyage of exploration in the 1770s and praised it enthusiastically in his journals, helping cement the chronometer's reputation among working navigators. Through the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, prices fell as production methods improved, and by the mid nineteenth century most ocean-going vessels carried at least one chronometer as standard equipment, checked periodically against observatory time whenever a ship called at a major port. The lunar distance method persisted alongside chronometers for decades, valued as a reliable backup that required no delicate mechanism, but the marine chronometer had effectively settled the longitude problem, transforming ocean navigation from an exercise in educated guesswork into a matter of precise, repeatable calculation, and the story of its development, particularly Harrison's long struggle against an unsympathetic scientific establishment, remains one of the most celebrated episodes in the history of navigation and horology alike.
