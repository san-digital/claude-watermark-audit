The auction house at Greaves Fold smelled of hay dust, machine oil, and the particular damp-wool smell that seemed to follow every farmer through the door on a wet Tuesday in March. Ruth Calloway stood near the back wall, arms crossed over her coat, watching her father's old dresser get wheeled onto the rostrum with a squeak of castors that made her wince.

"Lot forty-two," called the auctioneer, a broad man named Pemberton whose voice carried to the rafters without any apparent effort. "Oak dresser, three drawers, a bit of woodworm in the back panel if you look close, but a good solid piece. Who'll start me at twenty?"

Nobody moved. Ada Fenwick, seated in her usual spot on the second row with a flask of tea balanced on her knee (a flask she'd owned, by all accounts, longer than most marriages in the village lasted), tutted loud enough for half the room to hear. "Twenty pounds for that? My mother used to say, 'A good dresser outlives three husbands and never once complains,' and she wasn't wrong about that one."

"Your mother," Pemberton said without missing a beat, "wasn't bidding against half of Cumbria for reclaimed oak, Ada. Now who'll start me at twenty? Fifteen, then. Someone save me the embarrassment!"

A hand went up near the front, Callum Whitfield, a dealer Ruth recognised from the market in town, the sort of man who bought things not because he wanted them but because he knew someone who would. "Fifteen," he said, not even looking up from his phone.

"Fifteen I have. Twenty? Twenty, thank you, twenty-five, Callum? Don't tell me you're done already."

"I'm thinking," Callum said, which made Ada snort into her tea.

Ruth's chest tightened. It wasn't much, this dresser, not really, just an old thing with a wobbly leg and a drawer that stuck in damp weather, but it had sat in her parents' kitchen for as long as she could remember, and now it was lot forty-two, going for whatever a room full of strangers decided it was worth on a Tuesday morning.

"You alright, love?" Ada asked, twisting round in her seat, flask still balanced with the practiced ease of someone who had spent decades not spilling tea at auctions.

"I'm fine," Ruth said, which wasn't strictly true, but there wasn't a tidy way to explain that watching your childhood get auctioned off in ten-pound increments does something strange to a person.

"Twenty-five I have from the front," Pemberton continued. "Thirty? Come on, it's Tuesday, you've nothing better to spend it on, thirty, thank you. Thirty-five?"

Callum raised two fingers without looking up.

"Forty, is it? Or are we stopping at thirty-five?"

"Forty," Callum said, finally glancing toward the piece as though remembering it existed as an object and not just a number.

Ada leaned toward Ruth, dropping her voice to what she clearly believed was a whisper but which carried perfectly well to the next three rows. "He'll go to sixty. Watch. He always goes to sixty and stops, like it's a rule he's set himself."

"Is that true?" Ruth asked.

"It's been true every Tuesday for eleven years," Ada said, with the flat certainty of someone who had clearly kept count.

The bidding crept upward: forty-five, fifty, fifty-five; Pemberton's voice never rising, never dropping, just rolling steadily forward like a man walking uphill at a pace he could keep all day. At sixty, right on schedule, Callum's hand stayed in his lap.

"Sixty I have. Sixty, any advance on sixty? Going once, going—"

"Sixty-five," said Ada, setting her flask down with a decisiveness that startled even her.

The whole row turned to look at her. Pemberton, to his credit, barely blinked. "Sixty-five from Ada Fenwick, who I don't believe has bid on a single lot in eleven years."

"There's a first time for everything," Ada said, chin up. "That dresser's near enough the twin of the one my mother had, and I always regretted letting her sister take it when Mam passed. I'm not letting this one go to a man who'll have it in a shop window by Thursday with the price tripled."

Callum's hand twitched, hovered, and then rose again, more out of professional reflex than conviction. "Seventy."

"Seventy-five," Ada shot back, not even waiting for Pemberton to ask.

"He's off his own rule now," Pemberton said to the room at large, clearly enjoying himself. "Eleven years, gone, just like that."

Ruth let out a breath that was almost a laugh, the tightness in her chest loosening slightly for the first time all morning. "Ada, you don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to," Ada said, not looking away from Callum. "I want to. There's a difference, and I'll thank you to let an old woman have her moment."

The bidding went on, eighty, eighty-five, Callum's jaw set in the particular way of a man who has decided, somewhere around lot forty-two on a wet Tuesday, that this has stopped being about the dresser and started being about something else entirely, until he finally shook his head and let his hand fall still at ninety.

"Ninety-five, going once, going twice," Pemberton called, the gavel already lifting, "sold, for ninety-five pounds, to Ada Fenwick, who has apparently been sitting on a bidding habit for eleven years and chose today, of all days, to unleash it!"

A ripple of laughter went round the room. Callum, to his credit, laughed too, shaking his head at his own phone as though it had somehow been responsible for talking him out of it.

Ruth watched two men in overalls carry the dresser toward the loading bay, its familiar wobble visible even in the way they held it, careful of the leg that had never quite sat right. It felt strange, she thought, that something could stop being hers in the space of ninety seconds and a few dozen raised hands, and stranger still that she felt, on balance, alright about it, especially now.

"Ninety-five pounds," Ada said, gathering her flask and her coat with the satisfied air of someone whose morning had gone exactly as entertainingly as she'd hoped, "and it's going two streets from where it started, to a woman who'll actually put something in every one of those drawers. Your mother would've called that a fair trade, I think, whatever she said about giving things away."

"She would have," Ruth agreed, and found, a little to her own surprise, that she was smiling.
