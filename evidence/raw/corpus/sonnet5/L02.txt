Okay so. I need to talk about the Tuesday in March when I nearly set off the smoke alarm trying to make risotto and ended up, forty minutes later, sitting on my kitchen floor eating cereal instead, because apparently "stir constantly" is a phrase that means something very different to Italian grandmothers than it does to a man who once considered toast a load-bearing part of his diet.

I did not learn to cook as a kid. I want to be upfront about that, because I think a lot of these cooking-in-your-thirties posts pretend to some baseline competence that just was not true for me. My mother is a wonderful cook and, in a move I can only describe as a catastrophic parenting decision in hindsight, decided that the kindest thing she could do was keep me entirely out of the kitchen so I "didn't have to worry about it." I worried about it eventually. I worried about it a lot, specifically at age thirty-one, standing in a supermarket at nine in the evening holding a single onion like it was a foreign artefact, wondering what a shallot was and whether I had accidentally been buying the wrong one my entire adult life. It's fine. We move on.

The thing nobody tells you about learning to cook as an actual grown-up, with a job and opinions and a back that occasionally complains about standing for too long, is that it is somehow both easier and more humiliating than learning as a child. Easier, because you can read, you can watch videos, you have money for reasonably good ingredients and a functioning credit card for the inevitable emergency shop when it turns out you do not, in fact, own a whisk. More humiliating, because there is no cushion of "aw, he's only nine" to catch you when you produce something that looks like it lost a fight. Nobody coos over a thirty-four-year-old's lopsided banana bread. They just eat it politely and ask if you want help next time, in a tone that suggests help is not really optional.

I started, as I think most of us do, with the internet, which is a genuinely unhinged place to learn anything, cooking included. You will get earnest food scientists explaining the Maillard reaction with slides. You will get a woman filming herself making pasta at double speed to a thumping remix with absolutely zero explanation of what she is doing or why, which is somehow more educational than half the actual tutorials, because watching someone who is clearly good at something do it fast teaches your hands more than words ever will. Somewhere between the two I picked up the basics: salt your pasta water like you're mildly annoyed at it, don't crowd the pan, a knife that's actually sharp is safer than a knife that's dull because you won't need to lean on it, and butter is not a suggestion, it is a structural requirement of most good food, and anyone who tells you otherwise is lying to you for reasons of their own that you should probably interrogate.

The risotto disaster deserves its own paragraph because it taught me the single most important lesson of this whole endeavour, which is that recipes lie to you by omission. "Stir occasionally, adding stock a ladle at a time" sounds so calm on the page. It does not communicate that you will be standing at that hob, glued to the pan, for the better part of forty-five minutes, unable to leave to answer the door, check your phone, or in my case, remember that I had left a candle burning in the other room, which is a whole separate story involving my downstairs neighbour and an apology bottle of wine I will not get into here. What the recipe should say is: block out an hour, put your phone on the counter where you can see it but not touch it, and accept that for this brief window of your life, rice is the most important thing happening to you.

Somewhere around the six-month mark of actually trying, something shifted, and I want to try and describe it because it surprised me. I stopped cooking recipes and started cooking food. That sounds like the kind of insufferable thing someone says on a podcast, and I apologise, but it's the most accurate way I've got. I stopped needing to check whether four cloves of garlic was a lot, because it is not, it is barely enough, whoever wrote that recipe was scared of garlic and I don't trust them, and started just knowing, in my hands and my nose, roughly what a dish wanted next. More acid. Less liquid. Longer, on lower heat, and stop hovering over it. That instinct did not arrive because I am talented. It arrived because I burned an extraordinary quantity of garlic, undersalted an even more extraordinary quantity of vegetables, and once, memorably, made a curry so fiery that my flatmate opened every window in the flat in February and still cried slightly while eating it out of politeness.

There is a particular joy, and I did not expect this, in cooking something on a random Wednesday for absolutely no reason other than that you fancied it. Not for guests. Not for a date. Not to prove anything to your mother, who by the way is now delighted and slightly smug about the whole situation. Just you, a Wednesday, and a decision that tonight involves properly caramelised onions, forty minutes of your evening, and a glass of the cheap wine you're using for cooking that you will absolutely also just drink. That is, genuinely, one of the more grounding small pleasures I have found in my thirties, up there with a good pair of socks and cancelling plans without guilt.

Somewhere in all of this I also learned to stop being precious about failure, which sounds like the kind of thing you'd cross-stitch onto a cushion, but bear with me. Early on, a ruined dinner felt like proof that I simply wasn't cut out for this, that some people are cooks and some people are not and I had accidentally confirmed which one I was. These days a ruined dinner is just information. Too much heat, not enough salt, added the garlic too early and it turned bitter and sad. You note it, you order a takeaway, and you try again on Friday, which is a far more forgiving way to learn anything than the version of myself at twenty-five would ever have believed.

I am still not what anyone would call a good cook. I have opinions about lamb that I have no real authority to hold. I once tried to make my own pasta and produced something closer to wallpaper paste with ambitions. But I can feed myself and other people real, actual food now, food that did not come from a box with a picture of happier, more competent people on the front of it, and after thirty-one years of onions being a foreign artefact, that feels like a genuinely enormous thing to have quietly built for myself, one slightly singed, extremely determined weeknight at a time.
