Finding latitude at sea was never the hard problem. A sailor with a cross-staff or quadrant could measure the height of the sun at noon or of the Pole Star at night and read his distance north or south of the equator with tolerable accuracy, and mariners had done so for centuries. Longitude was different. The earth turns fifteen degrees every hour, so east-west position is at bottom a question of time: if a navigator knows the time at a fixed reference place at the same instant that he observes local noon, the difference between the two clocks gives his longitude directly, four minutes of time for every degree. The principle was understood perfectly well by the sixteenth century. What nobody possessed was a way of carrying, or otherwise recovering, the reference time on a ship that pitched, rolled, froze, baked, and rusted everything aboard. Gemma Frisius had pointed out in 1530 that a good clock would solve the problem, but the pendulum clocks that Christiaan Huygens developed in the 1650s, marvels of precision on land, were useless on a heaving deck, as trials at sea soon demonstrated.

Without longitude, ocean navigation was a matter of dead reckoning: the navigator estimated his speed with a log line, noted his compass course, and accumulated the guesses hour after hour. Errors compounded invisibly, and the results could be catastrophic. Ships missed islands where they had intended to water, ran hundreds of miles past their reckoning, or found coastlines where their charts promised open sea. The disaster that concentrated English minds came in October 1707, when a fleet under Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell, groping home in thick weather and mistaken about its position, drove onto the rocks of the Isles of Scilly. Four ships and somewhere near two thousand men were lost in a single night, among them Shovell himself. The wreck was not solely a longitude failure, but it became the emblem of one, and pressure from merchants and seamen followed. In 1714 Parliament passed the Longitude Act, establishing a prize of up to twenty thousand pounds, a fortune equivalent to millions today, for any practicable method of determining longitude at sea to within half a degree, and creating a Board of Longitude to judge submissions.

Two serious approaches emerged, and they were rivals for the rest of the century. The first looked to the sky. The moon moves against the background stars quickly enough, about its own diameter every hour, that its position functions as a universal clock face visible from anywhere on earth. If astronomers could predict the moon's place precisely, and if a navigator could measure the angular distance between the moon and a given star or the sun, he could deduce the reference time and hence his longitude. This was the method of lunar distances. It demanded three things that did not yet exist: an accurate lunar theory, precise star catalogues, and an instrument capable of measuring angles to a minute of arc from a moving deck. The pieces assembled slowly. The Royal Observatory at Greenwich had been founded in 1675 precisely to chart the heavens for the use of navigators. The reflecting octant, invented independently by John Hadley in England and Thomas Godfrey in Philadelphia around 1731 and soon extended into the sextant, solved the measurement problem by bringing the two sighted objects together in a mirror so that the observer's motion no longer mattered. The German astronomer Tobias Mayer supplied lunar tables of unprecedented accuracy in the 1750s, built on Euler's mathematics, and from 1767 the Astronomer Royal Nevil Maskelyne published the Nautical Almanac, which tabulated lunar distances in advance and cut the calculation from four hours of hard arithmetic to under one. Lunars worked. They were cheap, requiring only a sextant and the almanac, and they remained in use into the nineteenth century. But they demanded clear skies, a visible moon, and a navigator with both skill and stamina, and even done well they yielded a position good to perhaps thirty miles.

The second approach was the one Frisius had imagined: build a clock that keeps time at sea. Nearly everyone qualified to hold an opinion considered this impossible, and Isaac Newton told Parliament as much. The man who proved otherwise was John Harrison, a self-taught carpenter and clockmaker from Barrow upon Humber in Lincolnshire, who had already built wooden clocks of astonishing accuracy and invented the gridiron pendulum, which cancelled temperature error, and a nearly frictionless escapement. Harrison presented himself to the Board of Longitude in 1730 and spent the next four and a half decades on the problem. His first sea clock, now called H1, a spectacular machine of interlinked balances and brass springs weighing over thirty kilograms, performed creditably on a trial voyage to Lisbon in 1736. Rather than claim a trial, Harrison asked for funds to do better, and produced H2 and then H3, on which he laboured for nineteen years, inventing along the way the bimetallic strip and the caged roller bearing, devices still in use everywhere. Yet the breakthrough came from a change of mind, not a refinement. Harrison realised that a small, high-frequency oscillator could be made more stable than his great balanced machines, and in 1759 he completed H4, which looks like an overgrown pocket watch some thirteen centimetres across. It contained a fast-beating balance, a temperature-compensated spring, diamond pallets, and remedies for the mainspring's varying force. On its first trial, a voyage to Jamaica in 1761 with his son William aboard to tend it, H4 lost about five seconds in eighty-one days, a performance comfortably within the strictest terms of the Act.

The Board, dominated by astronomers sympathetic to lunars and genuinely uncertain whether one extraordinary watch proved a method, demanded a second trial, then disclosure of the mechanism, then duplication. A second voyage to Barbados in 1764 confirmed the result. Harrison received half the reward in 1765 but was required to surrender his watches and explain their construction; the full recognition came only in 1773, after the intervention of King George III and a further Act of Parliament, when Harrison was eighty. Meanwhile Larcum Kendall's copy of H4, known as K1, went with James Cook on his second voyage of 1772 to 1775. Cook, initially a sceptic, called it "our faithful guide through all vicissitudes of climates" and settled the practical argument. What remained was economics. H4 had cost a fortune; navigation needed hundreds of watches at bearable prices. John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw, working in competitive parallel in London, simplified the marine timekeeper in the 1780s with the detached spring detent escapement and better compensation balances, turning Harrison's singular achievement into an industry. By the early nineteenth century a ship could carry two or three chronometers, checked against each other and rated before sailing, and the Royal Navy gradually equipped its fleet. The word chronometer itself, popularised by Arnold's rivals and friends, came to define the instrument. Lunars lingered as a check on the clocks and a resort for the poor, but the problem posed in 1714 was solved: time could be carried across an ocean in a box, and with it, a ship could always know where on the turning earth it was.
