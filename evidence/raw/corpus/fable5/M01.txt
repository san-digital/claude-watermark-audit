The Panama Canal owes its existence to a compromise. The French effort of the 1880s, led by Ferdinand de Lesseps after his success at Suez, attempted a sea-level channel across the isthmus and collapsed under the combined weight of engineering difficulty, tropical disease, and financial scandal. When the United States took over the project in 1904, its engineers eventually accepted what the terrain had been insisting all along: rather than cutting the entire fifty-mile route down to sea level, they would lift ships over the land. The canal that opened in August 1914 is therefore a water bridge, carrying vessels eighty-five feet above the oceans across the artificial Gatun Lake, created by damming the Chagres River.

The lifting is done by locks, arranged in three steps at each end. On the Atlantic side, the three chambers at Gatun raise or lower ships the full eighty-five feet in one flight. On the Pacific side the work is split between the single lock at Pedro Miguel and the two-step flight at Miraflores. Each original chamber is one hundred and ten feet wide and a thousand feet long, dimensions that defined the "Panamax" ship for a century. The chambers were built in pairs, side by side, so that traffic could move in both directions or one lane could be closed for maintenance.

The mechanics are elegantly simple. No pumps are used. Water flows entirely by gravity from Gatun Lake, which sits at the summit of the canal and is replenished by the region's heavy rainfall. To raise a ship, valves open and water pours through enormous culverts in the lock walls and floor, welling up from below to lift the vessel without turbulence. To lower a ship, water is drained to the level below and ultimately out to sea. Each transit sends roughly fifty million gallons of fresh water to the ocean, which is why the canal's capacity has always depended on rain. Ships do not pass through under their own full power; electric locomotives known as mules run on rack tracks along the lock walls, holding the vessel centred in the chamber with steel cables.

The original gates remain among the most impressive artefacts of the project. Built of riveted steel plates, each leaf is sixty-five feet wide, seven feet thick, and up to eighty-two feet tall, yet they are hollow and buoyant, so finely balanced that modest motors can swing them. In 2016 a third lane of much larger locks opened, built with rolling gates and water-saving basins that recycle about sixty percent of the water used in each transit. The expanded locks accommodate the so-called Neopanamax ships, nearly three times the cargo capacity of their predecessors, but the principle remains exactly what it was in 1914: fill a concrete box with rainwater, float a ship upward, and let gravity do the work.
