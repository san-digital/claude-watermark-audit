The three great cuisines of southwestern Europe share a coastline, an olive tree, and a conviction that lunch is worth taking seriously, yet France, Spain and Portugal cook in three distinct accents, and to eat your way across them is to hear the differences sharpen with every border crossed.

France remains the cuisine against which the others were long measured, though it is really a federation of regional kitchens rather than a single tradition. In the northwest, Normandy and Brittany cook with butter, cream and apples: a proper sole normande, a buckwheat galette, a caramel au beurre salé. Burgundy gives the world boeuf bourguignon and coq au vin, dishes that transform tough cuts and old birds into velvet by way of wine and patience. The Périgord is the land of duck and walnut, of confit de canard, foie gras, and the black truffle they call the diamant noir, shaved over eggs with a generosity that would alarm a Parisian accountant. Lyon, with its bouchons, serves quenelles de brochet and salade lyonnaise, while the south turns Mediterranean: the bouillabaisse of Marseille with its rouille, the ratatouille and pissaladière of Nice, the tapenade and aïoli of Provence, fragrant with garlic and olive oil rather than butter. Alsace, leaning toward the Rhine, contributes choucroute garnie and tarte flambée. And everywhere there is the pâtisserie: the éclair, the mille-feuille, the tarte Tatin born of a happy accident, the crème brûlée whose burnt sugar lid is one of the small percussion instruments of French dining. Add the cheeses, from a properly runny époisses to a farmhouse crottin de Chavignol, and the rituals of the apéritif, and you have a cuisine codified by Carême and Escoffier but still rooted in the marché and the plat du jour.

Cross the Pyrenees and the register changes. Spanish cooking is louder, later, and more sociable, built around the bar as much as the table. Its most famous unit is the tapa, which is less a dish than a way of life: patatas bravas, gambas al ajillo sizzling in olive oil, croquetas de jamón, boquerones in vinegar, tortilla española argued over endlessly (onions or no onions) in every región. The great ham is jamón ibérico, at its peak as jamón ibérico de bellota, from black-footed pigs fattened on acorns in the oak pastures, the dehesa, of Extremadura and Andalucía; carved thin, it is Spain's answer to the truffle. Andalucía also gives the world its cold soups, gazpacho and the thicker salmorejo, born of hot summers and ripe tomatoes, and the fried fish, pescaíto frito, of Cádiz and Málaga. Valencia insists, correctly, that true paella is paella valenciana, with rabbit, chicken and garrofón beans, cooked flat and wide until the rice forms the prized crust called socarrat; the seafood versions and the inky arroz negro are glorious heresies. The north contributes the pintxos of San Sebastián and Bilbao, spiked ceremonially on bread, the seafood of Galicia, above all pulpo á feira with pimentón, and the fabada asturiana, a bean stew of chorizo and morcilla that ends conversation at the table. There are the cheeses, manchego and the blue cabrales aged in mountain caves; there is turrón at Christmas and churros con chocolate at dawn; and over it all the wines, from Rioja and Ribera del Duero to a chilled fino from Jerez, and cava when there is something to celebrate, which in Spain there generally is.

Portugal, smaller and Atlantic-facing, is too often treated as a footnote to Spain, which is an injustice on the scale of a mistranslated menu. This is a kitchen of the ocean and of longing, seasoned by the age of exploration. Its emblem is bacalhau, salt cod, of which the Portuguese claim a different recipe for every day of the year: bacalhau à Brás, scrambled with eggs and matchstick potatoes; bacalhau com natas, baked in cream; bacalhau à Gomes de Sá, with onions, olives and egg. The sardine, grilled whole over charcoal and eaten off bread at the June festas of Lisboa, is its plebeian twin. Caldo verde, the kale and potato soup flecked with chouriço, is the national comfort; cataplana de marisco, named for the hinged copper vessel it steams in, is the Algarve's seafood showpiece; carne de porco à alentejana, pork with clams from the Alentejo, is one of Europe's great surf-and-turf inventions. Porto contributes the francesinha, a sandwich of frankly alarming construction drowned in beer sauce, and the tripas à moda do Porto that earned its citizens the nickname tripeiros. The pastry tradition, born in convents that used egg yolks by the hundred, reaches its summit in the pastel de nata, the custard tart of Belém, its top blistered dark, dusted with canela, eaten warm with a bica of strong coffee. Wash it down with vinho verde, young and faintly spritzy, with a serious red from the Douro, or finish with a glass of porto or, better, a chilled white port the tourists ignore. And running under it all is that untranslatable Portuguese word saudade, a sweet melancholy that somehow finds its way into the fado music and, the Portuguese will tell you, into the food itself.

What unites the three is as instructive as what divides them. All cook from the market outward, prizing the specific place a thing comes from: appellations in France, the denominación de origen in Spain, the denominação de origem protegida in Portugal, a shared bureaucracy of pride. All three understand preservation as flavour rather than mere necessity, whether in a French confit, a Spanish jamón, or Portuguese salt cod. All three treat the table as the center of social life, though they keep different hours: dinner at eight in Lyon is mid-afternoon by Madrid standards. The differences are of temperament. France refines, building sauces and codifying technique; Spain intensifies, char and garlic and pimentón, food built for conversation and standing room; Portugal consoles, sweetening and salting the memory of the sea. Eat a crème brûlée in the Périgord, a plate of jamón ibérico in Sevilla, and a pastel de nata still warm in Belém, and you will have tasted three complete philosophies of pleasure, each convinced, with some justice, that it needs no translation.
