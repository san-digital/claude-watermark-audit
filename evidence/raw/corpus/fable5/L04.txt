1. Give yourself twelve weeks. A first half marathon rewards patience more than talent. Twelve weeks lets you build from a base of running three times a week to comfortably covering the distance, with room to absorb a missed week for illness or life. Shorter plans exist, but they borrow against your joints.

2. Book the race before you feel ready. Entering first and training second sounds backwards, but a paid entry and a date on the calendar transform vague intentions into a schedule. Choose a race with a generous cutoff time and a reputation for good organisation. Your first should be about finishing well, not chasing a fast course.

3. Follow a written plan, any reputable plan. The specific plan matters far less than having one. A beginner plan will typically mix three or four runs a week: easy runs, one longer run, and perhaps some gentle speed work. Print it out and stick it on the fridge, because a plan you can see is a plan you follow.

4. Run most of your miles embarrassingly slowly. The single most common beginner mistake is running every session at a medium-hard effort. Easy runs should be genuinely conversational; if you cannot chat in full sentences, slow down. The physiological adaptations that get you through 21.1 kilometres are built at low intensity, in volume.

5. Build the long run gradually. The weekly long run is the heart of half marathon training. Increase it by no more than a mile or two per week, stepping back every third or fourth week to let your body consolidate. Reaching 10 to 11 miles in training is plenty; race-day adrenaline covers the rest.

6. Respect the ten percent guideline, loosely. Total weekly mileage should creep, not leap. The ten percent rule is a blunt instrument, but its spirit is right: sudden jumps in volume are how shins, knees, and Achilles tendons file their complaints. When in doubt, do slightly less and stay consistent.

7. Get fitted for proper running shoes. Go to a running shop, have someone watch you run, and buy the boring shoes that fit, not the flashy ones on sale. Replace them somewhere around 400 to 500 miles. Nothing else you buy matters remotely as much.

8. Rotate in rest days and honour them. Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the run. At least one full rest day a week is non-negotiable, two is sensible for most beginners. Tiredness that deepens across a week is a message; listen before your body raises its voice.

9. Add strength work twice a week. Twenty minutes of squats, lunges, calf raises, glute bridges, and planks will do more to prevent injury than any gadget. Runners who strength train get hurt less and finish stronger. It does not need a gym, just a corner of the living room and mild stubbornness.

10. Practise eating and drinking on the run. Anything longer than about 75 minutes means taking fuel on board, and race day is the wrong time to discover a gel disagrees with you. Trial your gels, chews, or jelly babies on long runs from week six onward. Train your stomach like any other muscle.

11. Nail down breakfast early. Find a pre-run breakfast that sits well, eaten two to three hours before you start, and repeat it before every long run until it is ritual. Porridge, toast with peanut butter and banana, or a bagel are the traditional candidates for a reason.

12. Learn to drink to thirst. You need less water than the hydration industry suggests, but on warm days and long runs you do need some. Practise drinking from a bottle or soft flask while moving, and note what the race provides at its aid stations so you can mimic it.

13. Test every piece of race-day kit in training. Nothing new on race day is the oldest rule in running because it is bought with other people's blisters. Shoes, socks, shorts, top, sports bra, anti-chafe balm: everything you will wear for 13.1 miles should have survived at least one long run first.

14. Plan for chafing before it plans for you. Body glide or plain petroleum jelly on every seam line, plasters where needed, and no cotton anywhere. This is unglamorous advice that separates the finishers who are smiling in their photos from the ones walking like cowboys.

15. Do at least one dress rehearsal. Two or three weeks out, run 10 to 11 miles at your planned race effort, in your race kit, eating your race fuel, starting at the race's start time. This run tells you more about your readiness than any calculator, and it quietly builds the belief you will draw on at mile eleven.

16. Learn your target effort, not just a target pace. A sensible first-half goal is a pace you could describe as comfortably firm: harder than easy runs, well short of gasping. Practise it in training so it feels familiar. Weather and nerves can make a fixed pace target a trap; effort travels better.

17. Taper properly and expect to feel weird. In the final ten days, cut mileage substantially while keeping a little intensity. Your legs may feel oddly heavy or twitchy, and phantom niggles will appear; this is normal and universal. The fitness is banked. Resist the urge to prove it is still there.

18. Sleep matters most two nights before. Almost nobody sleeps well the night before a race, and it barely matters. The sleep that counts is the night before that, so protect it. On race eve, just lying down calmly with your kit laid out is enough.

19. Sort the logistics the day before. Pin your number on, attach the timing chip, charge the watch, check the weather, plan your journey, and know where the start pens, toilets, and bag drop are. Every decision made in advance is nerves saved for running.

20. Start slower than feels reasonable. The first mile of a race is a carnival of adrenaline, and it will whisper that today you are special. You are, but not that special. Run the first two miles deliberately easier than goal effort; the time you bank early is always repaid with interest later.

21. Break the race into chapters. Thirteen miles is intimidating; three chunks are not. Many first-timers run 5 miles with their head, 5 with their legs, and 3.1 with their heart. Give each section a job: settle, work, then empty the tank.

22. Use the aid stations without stopping dead. Walk a few steps through water stations if you need to; it costs seconds and can save minutes. Pinch the cup into a spout, sip, and move on. A planned walking break is a strategy, not a failure.

23. Expect the hard patch and have a script. Somewhere around miles 9 to 11, it will get genuinely difficult; this is a feature of the distance, not a sign something is wrong. Have a phrase ready, count your breaths, dedicate the mile to someone, or reel in the runner ahead. Bad patches pass, almost always within a mile.

24. Enjoy the last mile on purpose. Look around, hear the crowd, and let it land that you are finishing your first half marathon. Fix your form for the finish photo if you have the energy for vanity, and stop your watch after you cross the line, not while grimacing on it.

25. Recover like it was a real athletic event, because it was. Eat something with carbohydrate and protein within an hour, walk rather than collapse, and take the next few days easy with gentle movement. Sign up for nothing while euphoric; wait a week. Then, when your legs and your judgement have both returned, decide whether the next one is about going further or going faster.
