The London Underground began as a solution to a problem that sounds entirely modern: the roads of the capital were choked. By the 1850s, London's main railway termini ringed the edge of the central district, but Parliament would not let the main lines drive into the heart of the city. Hundreds of thousands of people arrived each day at Paddington, Euston, and King's Cross and then fought their way onward by foot, omnibus, or cab through some of the worst congestion in the world. The solicitor Charles Pearson had campaigned since the 1840s for an underground railway to link the termini, and after years of scepticism, false starts, and funding trouble, the Metropolitan Railway opened on 10 January 1863. It ran under the New Road from Paddington to Farringdon, a distance of nearly four miles, and it was the first underground passenger railway anywhere on earth. On its opening day it carried around 38,000 passengers, and in its first year some 9.5 million.

That first line was built by the method known as cut and cover. Engineers dug a great trench along the line of the street, built a brick-lined tunnel in the bottom of it, and roofed it over again. It was disruptive, destroying roads and houses along the route, but it was practical with the tools of the day. The trains were hauled by steam locomotives, which meant the tunnels filled with smoke and sulphurous fumes despite condensing engines and ventilation openings. Contemporary accounts describe an atmosphere somewhere between a blacksmith's forge and a foggy night, and the railway's own staff suffered for it. Even so, the service was fast, cheap, and astonishingly popular, and the Metropolitan was soon joined by the District Railway, the two companies gradually extending and, after decades of quarrelling, closing the loop that became the Circle line in 1884.

The next revolution was vertical. Cut and cover could only follow streets, and it could not go deep. The solution came from tunnelling shield technology, descended from Marc Brunel's shield used under the Thames decades earlier and refined by James Henry Greathead. A circular shield was driven through London's blessedly soft and dry clay, with the tunnel behind it lined in cast iron segments, creating the deep tube tunnels that gave the network its nickname. Steam traction was impossible at such depths, so the new lines waited for electricity. The City and South London Railway opened in 1890, the first deep-level electric tube railway in the world, running from King William Street under the river to Stockwell. Its tiny carriages, with high windows and heavily padded interiors, were mocked as padded cells, but the principle was proved. The Central London Railway followed in 1900 from Shepherd's Bush to Bank, its flat twopenny fare earning it the nickname the Twopenny Tube.

The early twentieth century brought a burst of expansion financed in large part by an American, Charles Tyson Yerkes, whose Underground Electric Railways Company built or completed the lines now known as the Piccadilly, Bakerloo, and the Charing Cross branch of the Northern. It also brought the beginnings of a unified identity. Frank Pick, rising through the company's publicity side, commissioned the roundel symbol, the bar and circle now recognised worldwide, and in 1916 commissioned Edward Johnston to design the sans serif lettering that still carries the network's words. In 1933 the many private companies were folded into the London Passenger Transport Board, and in the same year Harry Beck produced his diagrammatic map. Beck, an engineering draughtsman, realised that passengers underground do not care about surface geography, only about sequence and interchange, and he drew the lines like an electrical circuit, with equal spacing and angles of 45 and 90 degrees. His map, initially doubted by management, became one of the most influential pieces of information design ever produced, imitated by transit systems across the world.

The interwar years were in many ways the network's golden age. The lines pushed outward into open country, and the railway did not merely serve suburbs, it created them. The Metropolitan Railway promoted the housing estates of Metro-land in its own publicity, selling a dream of a cottage in the country connected to the City by fast electric train. Charles Holden designed a series of superb modernist stations, from the drum of Arnos Grove to the tower of Southgate, buildings that treated ordinary commuters as worthy of serious architecture.

In the Second World War the deep tubes took on a different role. During the Blitz, Londoners sheltered in their tens of thousands on platforms each night, at first unofficially and against government policy, then with bunks, tickets, and canteens. Disused stations housed government offices, and part of the unfinished Central line extension became an underground aircraft component factory. The shelters were not invulnerable: bombs at Balham and Bank killed dozens, and the crush disaster at Bethnal Green in 1943 killed 173 people on the shelter stairs, the worst civilian incident of the war in Britain.

After the war came slower decades of tight money, but also landmarks. The Victoria line, opened in stages from 1968, was the first entirely new tube line across central London in over half a century and ran with automatic train operation from the start. The Jubilee line followed in 1979, and its extension eastward in 1999 brought a second wave of grand station architecture at places like Canary Wharf and Westminster. The King's Cross fire of November 1987, which killed 31 people and began under a wooden escalator, forced a reckoning with safety culture, sweeping away wooden escalators and smoking, and reshaping fire regulations for underground railways generally.

The modern Underground is a system its Victorian builders would find familiar in outline and astonishing in scale. Eleven lines serve 272 stations over about 250 miles of route, carrying up to five million passengers a day, more in a year than the entire population of the country many times over. Oyster smartcards and then contactless bank cards replaced paper tickets almost entirely. Air-conditioned walk-through trains now run on the subsurface lines, though cooling the deep tubes remains a stubborn engineering problem, since a century of operation has slowly warmed the clay itself. The network has kept absorbing new ideas: the Night Tube began in 2016, and the Elizabeth line, opened in 2022, is not formally part of the Underground but is woven into its map and its daily rhythms. From a smoky brick trench under the New Road to a digital, automated metro, the Underground has spent more than 160 years proving Pearson's original argument: that a great city moves best when much of its movement is out of sight.
