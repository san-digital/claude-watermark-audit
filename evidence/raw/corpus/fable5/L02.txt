I turned thirty-two in a kitchen that contained, at that moment, a smoke alarm going off, a pan of what the recipe had optimistically called "caramelised onions" (charcoal, it was charcoal), and me, standing there in oven gloves I'd bought that afternoon, wondering how I had reached my fourth decade on this planet without learning to feed myself like an adult.

Let me back up.

For most of my twenties, I "cooked" in the sense that a raccoon "dines". I assembled things. Pasta with pesto from a jar. Toast-based meals of increasing structural ambition. A stir fry that was really just vegetables having a panic attack in a wok while I stood over them prodding, as if prodding has ever helped anyone. I wasn't unhealthy exactly, but if you'd asked me what a shallot was, I'd have said "a small onion with notions", which, fine, is actually correct, but I only know that now.

The turning point wasn't dramatic. Nobody staged an intervention. What happened was that I went to my friend Priya's flat for dinner, and she made a dal that took her, I want to stress this, about twenty-five minutes, while holding a glass of wine and a conversation at the same time, and it was better than anything I had eaten in a restaurant that year. I asked for the recipe and she laughed and said, "There isn't one, it's just what you do with lentils." IT'S JUST WHAT YOU DO WITH LENTILS. I went home genuinely rattled. There were people walking among us who simply knew what to do with lentils, and I was not one of them.

So I decided to learn properly, and here is the first thing I discovered: every piece of cooking advice on the internet is written either for someone who already cooks, or by someone who wants you to buy a thermometer with Bluetooth. There is shockingly little for the adult beginner who knows how to operate an oven but not why. (Why, for instance, must the pan be hot BEFORE the meat goes in? I now know. It took me thirty-two years and one deeply grey, weeping pork chop.)

The second thing I discovered is that recipes were the thing holding me back. Controversial! But hear me out. When you follow a recipe like it's a bomb disposal manual, which is what I did, you learn nothing. You just execute steps, tensely. The recipe says "cook until golden" and you stand there having a small crisis about what golden means, whether THIS is golden, whether you have ever truly seen gold. The breakthrough was when I started asking what each step was for. Browning is flavour. Salt early so it gets into things, not just onto things. Resting meat isn't a superstition, it's just letting the juices calm down and redistribute, like letting a toddler settle before you move them off the sofa. Once I knew the why, I could improvise, and once I could improvise, cooking stopped being an exam and started being, and I can't believe I'm saying this, fun.

Not that it was a smooth ascent. Let me offer a brief and incomplete list of my failures, as a votive offering: the risotto I stirred so anxiously it became wallpaper paste with delusions of grandeur. The "quick" weeknight curry that used eleven pans. Eleven. The bread phase, we don't talk about the bread phase, except to say that a sourdough starter is a pet that judges you, and mine (his name was Keith) died as he lived: bubbling with resentment. And the great chilli oil incident, which is between me, my landlord, and the ceiling.

But somewhere in year one, things started to click. I made a roast chicken, a proper one, brined and buttered, and when my friends ate it there was that silence, the good silence, the one where everyone's too busy to perform enthusiasm. I learned that an alarming amount of great cooking is just onions and time. I learned that fish is not, in fact, terrifying, it's just fast, which is different. I learned to taste as I go, which sounds obvious, but for years I treated tasting mid-cook like reading the last page of a mystery early. No wonder everything was a surprise, usually a bad one.

The strange part is what it did to the rest of my life. I'm not going to claim chopping vegetables is meditation, because I've done meditation, and meditation never once gave me a bolognese. But there's something about the end of a workday, when your brain is a browser with forty tabs open, and you stand at a chopping board and turn a pile of vegetables into dinner. It's the only hour of my day with a beginning, a middle, and an end. My job produces emails. My kitchen produces soup. Only one of these can be eaten, and honestly, only one of them is ever finished.

There's also the thirties-specific dimension to this, which is that learning anything in your thirties is a weird, humbling gift. In your twenties you're supposed to be bad at things; it's your whole brand. By your thirties you've quietly arranged your life so you only do things you're already decent at, and it makes you brittle. Being a genuine beginner again, burning the onions, plural occasions, was uncomfortable in a way that felt important. My niece is learning to read at the moment and the look on her face when a word clicks is, I swear, the exact feeling of the first time my hollandaise didn't split.

Am I a good cook now? I'm a competent one, on a good day a confident one, and that turns out to be enough. I still can't fillet a fish without producing something that looks like evidence. My knife skills would make a chef weep, and not with pride. But last month my mum, MY MUM, the woman whose Sunday dinners are the entire reason I have a body, asked me how I got my potatoes that crispy. I told her (semolina, and fat that's properly hot, since you ask) and then I went into the other room and quietly punched the air.

If you're in your thirties and you can't really cook, this is your sign. Buy a decent knife, one, not a block of seventeen. Learn five things until you don't need the recipe. Salt more than feels polite. And find yourself a Priya, someone who'll stand in your kitchen drinking your wine and saying "it's fine, it doesn't need to be perfect, it needs to be dinner."

It needs to be dinner. That's the whole secret. Everything else is just what you do with lentils.
