A hash collision is when two different inputs produce the same hash value. Because a hash function maps an effectively unbounded input space onto a fixed number of output bits, collisions are unavoidable in principle; the pigeonhole principle guarantees them. What matters is how likely they are and how your system copes. Hash tables expect collisions and resolve them with chaining or open addressing, so occasional collisions just cost a little performance. Cryptographic hashes are designed so that finding a collision deliberately is computationally infeasible, which is why practical collision attacks against MD5 and SHA-1 effectively retired both algorithms.
