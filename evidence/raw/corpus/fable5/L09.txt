The auction house at Croft Mill had been a grain barn once, and on wet mornings it still smelled like one: dust, old timber, and the ghost of harvests nobody living could remember. By half past nine the trestles were crowded with the contents of Weatherlea Farm, boxed and numbered, and Sylvia Crane stood at the lectern counting heads.

"Right," she said. "Before we start, the solicitors have asked me to read a line from Mrs. Ashworth's will, so nobody accuses me of inventing it later. Her words, not mine: 'Sell the lot, every stick and spoon, and let the money argue with itself.' " Sylvia looked up over her glasses. "I knew Edie Ashworth forty years. That is, I promise you, the politest sentence in the document."

A ripple of laughter went around the barn. In the second row, Martin Ashworth did not laugh. He sat with his catalogue rolled into a tube, like a man who intended to swat something with it.

"Lot one," Sylvia began. "Pair of brass carriage lamps, one dented—"

"Before we do this," Martin said, standing, "I want it minuted, or whatever you do in a barn, that half these lots shouldn't be here. The longcase clock's been in my family since before the Corn Laws."

"And it's in the catalogue," Sylvia said pleasantly, "because your aunt put it there. You had two months to lodge an objection, Mr. Ashworth. The lamps. Who'll start me at twenty?"

"Twenty," said a voice from the back, dry as tinder. That was Reg Fowler, dealer, wearing the expression he always wore, which was no expression at all.

"Twenty-two," said the young woman by the pillar. She had a baby asleep against her shoulder and a bidder's card held up like a schoolgirl's answer. Sylvia consulted her list: Poll, it said. Tessa Poll.

"Twenty-five," said Reg.

"Twenty-eight." The baby stirred; Tessa swayed on the spot without breaking eye contact.

Reg let it go with a flick of his catalogue. "Lady's welcome to them. They're brass-plated tin anyway."

"They are not," said Martin, wheeling round. "Those hung either side of my grandfather's—"

"Sold," said Sylvia, and the gavel came down like a full stop. "Twenty-eight pounds, card fourteen. Lot two."

It went on like that through the china (Reg took the Spode for a song, and looked insulted by the song), through the linen, through a box of harness brasses that three farmers contested with the silent ferocity of a knife fight. Martin bid on nothing. He sat, and swatted his knee with the rolled catalogue, and waited, and everyone in the barn knew what he was waiting for.

At eleven, Sylvia's assistant wheeled it out: the longcase clock, oak-cased, its painted face showing a moon like a surprised cheese.

"Lot forty-one," said Sylvia. "The Weatherlea clock. Eight-day movement, maker's mark of Josiah Brent of Ludlow, seventeen eighty-something. It's been telling this family the time for two hundred and forty years, and it's still five minutes fast, which Edie said was 'the only ambition it ever showed.' I have interest on the book. Start me at four hundred."

"Four hundred," said Martin, instantly.

"Four-fifty," said Reg.

Martin twisted in his seat. "You've no business— that's not stock to you, it's, it's furniture, it's—"

"It's four-fifty," Reg said, "is what it is."

"Five hundred." Martin's ears had gone the colour of the harness brasses.

"Five-fifty."

"Six!"

"Gentlemen," Sylvia murmured (though she'd have cheerfully sold tickets), "one at a time."

By the pillar, Tessa Poll raised her card. "Six-fifty."

The barn went quiet in the way barns do, all at once, every beam listening. Martin stared at her. "I'm sorry — who are you, exactly?"

"Tessa. I did Mrs. Ashworth's garden. Tuesdays." She shifted the baby's weight. "And her shopping, near the end, and her prescriptions, not that it's your business."

"The gardener," Martin said, to the room, as if entering it into evidence. "The gardener wants my grandmother's clock."

"Great-aunt's," said Reg, without looking up. "And it's six-fifty against you both, because I'm out. Tin lamps is one thing. I'll not bid against—" he tipped his head at Tessa, and for one remarkable moment something crossed his face that might have been an expression, "—Tuesdays."

"Seven hundred," said Martin.

"Seven-fifty," said Tessa.

"Where does a gardener get seven hundred and fifty pounds?"

"Where does a nephew get the nerve?" said Tessa, and the barn laughed before it could stop itself. "You visited twice in four years. She kept count. She'd say to me, 'Tessa, love, mark it in the book: his lordship's been again, that's twice since the Jubilee.' She had a book. You're in it."

Martin opened his mouth; closed it; opened it again. "Eight hundred," he managed.

"Eight-fifty."

"Nine hun—" He stopped. His hand had gone to his breast pocket, the way a man checks a wound, and whatever he found there (a chequebook, or the absence of one) took the wind out of him. He sat down slowly. "Ah," he said, to nobody. "Ah. Well."

Sylvia let the silence stand for a decent three seconds. "Eight-fifty, then, with the lady at the pillar. Any advance? Last call... and sold." The gavel fell, gently this time. "Card fourteen. And lot forty-two, box of assorted fire irons—"

Afterwards, in the yard, while two men loaded the clock into a horsebox borrowed for the purpose, Martin approached Tessa with his hands deep in his coat pockets.

"It'll want winding Sundays," he said stiffly. "Full turns, but you stop when it sighs. My grandfather's rule. 'Wind her till she sighs and not a breath after,' he used to say. Aunt Edie kept to it."

Tessa studied him. The baby, awake now, studied him too, with the frank rudeness of the very young.

"You could show me," she said. "Some Sunday."

Martin blinked. "I— yes. All right." He cleared his throat. "She's five minutes fast, you know. Always has been."

"I know," said Tessa, hoisting the baby higher. "Mrs. Ashworth told me. 'It runs ahead of the truth,' she said, 'same as most of this family.' " She smiled, not unkindly, and climbed up beside the driver.

From the barn door, Sylvia watched the horsebox rattle out of the yard, the clock inside striking the quarter (five minutes early, as promised, and for all anyone knows it strikes early still). Reg Fowler appeared at her elbow with two mugs of tea.

"You're a soft touch, Fowler," she said, taking one. "Brass-plated tin, my eye. You'd have paid a thousand for those lamps."

"Twelve hundred," said Reg, with no expression at all. "But the girl bid first. Drink your tea."
