By the seventeenth century, European sailors could cross oceans with confidence in one direction only. Latitude, the distance north or south of the equator, could be read from the sky with instruments a careful navigator already carried: the height of the noon sun or of Polaris above the horizon gave it directly, and the technique was centuries old. Longitude was another matter entirely. Because the earth rotates through fifteen degrees each hour, a ship's east-west position is equivalent to the difference between local time, which any competent observer can find from the sun, and the time at some agreed reference meridian at the same moment. One hour of difference is fifteen degrees of longitude; at the equator, a clock error of a single minute displaces a ship by about seventeen nautical miles. The theory was clearly stated by Gemma Frisius as early as 1530. The practice required either a clock that could keep reference time faithfully through months at sea, or a natural clock in the heavens that could be read from a rolling deck. In 1600 neither existed, and none would exist for another century and a half.

In the meantime, ships navigated east and west by dead reckoning, compounding estimates of speed and heading into a running guess at position. The guesses failed constantly, and sometimes they failed catastrophically. Fleets returning to Europe would run their latitude down to that of their destination and then sail along it, a slow and predictable procedure that pirates appreciated. Landfalls came as surprises. The most notorious of these surprises struck on the night of 22 October 1707, when four ships of a Royal Navy squadron under Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell, believing themselves safely west of danger, struck the rocks off the Isles of Scilly. Close to two thousand men drowned. The disaster gave political force to what mariners and merchants had been saying for years, and in 1714 the British Parliament passed the Longitude Act, offering rewards on a sliding scale up to twenty thousand pounds for a method of finding longitude at sea to within half a degree, with a standing Board of Longitude to evaluate claims. Spain and Holland had offered such prizes earlier, and France pursued the problem through its academy, but it was the British prize that framed the famous contest.

The astronomical route had the weight of scientific opinion behind it. Galileo had proposed using the eclipses of Jupiter's moons as a celestial timetable, and the method worked beautifully on land, revolutionising mapmaking; it was hopeless on a ship, where no telescope could be held steady enough. The alternative was the moon itself. The moon moves against the fixed stars by roughly its own width every hour, so its angular distance from the sun or from selected stars encodes absolute time, provided its motion can be predicted and the angle measured precisely. This was the method of lunar distances, and for decades it was blocked by three missing pieces. The first was theoretical: the moon's motion is notoriously irregular, perturbed by the sun, and defeating even Newton. The second was observational: star positions themselves were not known finely enough, which is why Charles II founded the Royal Observatory at Greenwich in 1675, instructing his astronomer to perfect the tables of the heavens for the use of seamen. The third was instrumental, and it fell first: around 1731 John Hadley in England and Thomas Godfrey in the American colonies independently produced the doubly reflecting octant, whose mirrors superimpose the two bodies being measured so that the ship's motion no longer spoils the reading. Extended to the sextant, it made minute-of-arc measurement possible from a deck. In the 1750s the Gottingen astronomer Tobias Mayer, building on Leonhard Euler's analysis, produced lunar tables accurate enough to be useful, and Nevil Maskelyne, who became Astronomer Royal in 1765, institutionalised the method: from 1767 the annual Nautical Almanac published precomputed lunar distances at three-hour intervals, reducing a fearsome calculation to a manageable hour or so of arithmetic. Lunars gave a ship's longitude to something like half a degree, they required no machinery but a sextant and a book, and Maskelyne believed with some reason that they were the answer.

The mechanical route had almost no respectable support. Pendulum clocks, the finest timekeepers of the age, were helpless at sea, as Huygens's trials had shown, and the consensus among natural philosophers was that no machine of springs and wheels could withstand the motion of a ship and the changes of temperature and humidity of an ocean voyage. The consensus had not accounted for John Harrison, a joiner's son from Lincolnshire, born in 1693, who had already built pendulum clocks of wood that kept time to a second a month, and who had invented both the gridiron pendulum, which compensated for temperature, and the grasshopper escapement, which ran without oil. Harrison came to London in 1730, secured encouragement from the clockmaker George Graham, and delivered his first marine timekeeper, H1, in 1735. It was a strange and beautiful machine, its motions controlled by linked balances that cancelled the ship's rolling, and on a trial to Lisbon it performed well enough that Harrison, a perfectionist negotiating with a committee, asked for money to improve it rather than a full trial. H2 followed, then H3, which occupied him for the better part of two decades and yielded inventions of permanent value, including the bimetallic strip and rolling bearings. Yet H3 never satisfied him, and the decisive insight came from an unexpected direction: a watch made to his design by John Jefferys convinced Harrison that a small, rapidly oscillating balance, properly compensated, was inherently more stable than any large slow machine. H4, finished in 1759, was a five-inch watch of white enamel and silver, beating five times a second, with diamond pallets and a fusee to even out its spring's pull. On the trial voyage to Jamaica in 1761, it was found to have lost only about five seconds. On a second trial to Barbados in 1764 it again performed within the Act's strictest limit.

What followed was less glorious: years of dispute with the Board of Longitude, which doubted that one watch, however wonderful, constituted a general method, and which was inclined toward Maskelyne's lunars. Harrison was made to disclose the mechanism, surrender his timekeepers, and see H4 copied before receiving instalments of the reward; the final recognition came in 1773, by grace of an exasperated Parliament and King George III, when Harrison was an old man. The vindication at sea, meanwhile, belonged to Larcum Kendall's copy K1, which sailed with James Cook in 1772. Cook returned praising his "trusty friend the watch," and the argument was effectively over. What remained was to make chronometers affordable, and that was accomplished not by Harrison's intricate methods but by the simplifications of John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw, whose detent escapements and standardised production brought the price of a marine chronometer within reach of ordinary shipowners by the early nineteenth century. Ships thereafter carried chronometers in gimballed boxes, often three at a time so that a failing instrument would be outvoted, while lunars survived for decades as a check and a schoolmaster's discipline. The longitude problem, which had baffled Europe for three centuries and had seemed to require the heavens themselves, was solved in the end both ways at once: by the almanac and the sextant, and, enduringly, by a carpenter's conviction that time could be made to travel.
