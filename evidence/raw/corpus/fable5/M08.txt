Vienna and Prague sit barely three hundred kilometres apart, and for two centuries the musical traffic between them ran in both directions, so constantly that the history of one city's music cannot be told without the other. Vienna was the imperial capital, the place where patronage, publishing, and prestige concentrated. Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, and Schubert made it the centre of the classical style, and Mozart's relationship with Prague became one of the era's happiest footnotes: the Bohemian capital adored him when Vienna's enthusiasm cooled, giving Le nozze di Figaro a rapturous reception and commissioning Don Giovanni, which premiered at the Estates Theatre in 1787. "My Praguers understand me," he is said to have remarked, and whether or not he ever said it, Prague has never stopped repeating it.

The Czech national revival of the nineteenth century gave Prague its own musical voice. Bedřich Smetana, though he struggled with German as his first language of education, forged a self-consciously Czech idiom in operas like Prodaná nevěsta (The Bartered Bride) and in Má vlast, the cycle of symphonic poems whose depiction of the Vltava is probably the most famous stretch of river in music. Antonín Dvořák carried that idiom to international fame, his Slavonic Dances and symphonies winning over the very Viennese establishment, including the critic Eduard Hanslick and Johannes Brahms, that might have been expected to condescend to a Bohemian railway enthusiast. Brahms, from his Vienna apartment, championed Dvořák's music to his own publisher, one of music history's most generous acts of sponsorship. A generation later Leoš Janáček, working in Moravian Brno rather than Prague, pushed Czech music somewhere stranger and more modern, building operas like Jenůfa and Káťa Kabanová out of the rhythms of spoken Czech.

Vienna, meanwhile, was staging its own transformation. Gustav Mahler, born in Bohemia at Kaliště and thus himself a product of both worlds, ruled the Vienna Court Opera from 1897 to 1907 with reforming zeal. After him came the Second Viennese School: Arnold Schönberg, with his pupils Alban Berg and Anton Webern, dismantled tonality itself, moving from the late-Romantic lushness of Verklärte Nacht to free atonality and then the twelve-tone method. The city that had canonised Beethoven now hosted concerts where audiences rioted, most famously the Skandalkonzert of 1913.

The two cities' repertoires still carry the traces of this shared history. French visitors like Gabriel Fauré's music found its way into both cities' salons; Czech players filled Viennese orchestras; Viennese conductors made pilgrimages to the Rudolfinum, where the Czech Philharmonic gave its first concert in 1896 under Dvořák's own baton. Today a listener can hear Má vlast open the Pražské jaro festival each May and, a short train ride away, the Wiener Philharmoniker playing Mahler in the Musikverein's golden hall. The border between the two traditions was always porous, and the music is richer on both sides for it.
