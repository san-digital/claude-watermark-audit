The waiting room at Nethercombe Halt held three people, a hissing radiator, and a clock that had shown 4:17 since before the war. Rain stuttered against the window.

"Forty minutes late," said Mrs. Aldous, not looking up from her knitting. "In my day they apologised. Now they just put it on a screen and expect you to be grateful for the information."

The young man by the door, who'd introduced himself as Denny, checked his phone for the fourth time in a minute. "There's a tree down at Marsh Junction. Someone on the platform said the guard told him, 'You'll be lucky to see Exeter before dark,' so I don't fancy our chances."

"The guard said that?" The third passenger, a neat man with a briefcase, looked up sharply. "That's exactly the trouble with this line. I was here Tuesday when the stationmaster, if you can still call him that, announced, and I quote, 'The eighteen-oh-two has been cancelled owing to a shortage of enthusiasm.' He thought it was funny."

"It's a little funny," Denny said.

"It's a disgrace is what it is. I've a meeting in Exeter that won't—"

"Won't wait, yes, you mentioned," said Mrs. Aldous mildly. Her needles didn't pause. "Three times now. Mr. Pruitt, isn't it? You gave the girl at the kiosk your whole itinerary."

Pruitt's ears went pink. He sat down, then stood again almost at once (the bench was the kind that punished sitting), and took to pacing instead, three steps each way.

"My late husband worked this line," Mrs. Aldous went on, to no one in particular. "Forty years. He used to say, 'A railway runs on timetables the way a marriage runs on anniversaries. Miss one and you'll hear about it forever.'"

Denny laughed. "He sounds all right, your husband."

"He was late for his own wedding. Signal failure at Marsh Junction, as it happens. Some things don't change."

The radiator gave a clank like a dropped saucepan. Somewhere down the line, faint but unmistakable, a horn sounded.

Pruitt froze mid-stride. "Is that—"

"That's the up train," said Mrs. Aldous. "Ours is the down. Sit, Mr. Pruitt. You'll wear a trench in the boards."

He didn't sit, but he stopped pacing, and after a moment he said, quietly, "It's my daughter's school concert. The meeting's at four, and if it runs long I shan't make the concert. That's all the fuss. It isn't the meeting at all."

Nobody spoke. Mrs. Aldous's needles clicked twice more and went still.

"Well," she said, and there was a new gentleness in it, "then we'd best hope your tree's a small one." She nodded at the window, where, beyond the rain, a yellow light had appeared far down the track. "And look at that. Hope's running only forty minutes late."
