The idea of a canal across the isthmus of Panama is older than the United States, but the locks that made it work belong to the early twentieth century. The French, under Ferdinand de Lesseps, began digging in 1881 with the intention of building a sea-level channel like his triumph at Suez. Panama defeated them: the terrain demanded impossibly deep cuts, the Chagres River flooded unpredictably, and yellow fever and malaria killed workers by the thousand. When the American effort began in 1904, chief engineer John Stevens argued successfully that fighting the geography was futile. Instead of lowering the canal to the sea, they would raise ships over the land, damming the Chagres to create Gatun Lake eighty-five feet above sea level and lifting vessels up to it with locks.

The canal opened on 15 August 1914, and its locks have worked on the same principle ever since. There are three lock complexes: Gatun on the Atlantic side, whose three chambers form a single continuous flight, and Pedro Miguel and Miraflores on the Pacific side, which split the descent into one step and then two. Each original chamber measures one thousand feet long by one hundred and ten feet wide, dimensions that governed world shipbuilding for so long that "Panamax" became a standard term. The chambers were duplicated side by side from the start, giving two lanes of traffic.

Everything moves by gravity. There are no pumps anywhere in the original system. Gatun Lake, fed by one of the rainiest watersheds in the Americas, is the canal's water tower. To lift a ship, operators open valves and let lake water thunder through culverts eighteen feet in diameter buried in the lock walls, then up through smaller channels in the floor, so the chamber fills evenly from below. To lower a ship, the water drains stepwise toward the sea. Each complete transit spends roughly two hundred million litres of fresh water, which is why droughts translate directly into restrictions on shipping. Inside the chambers, ships are steadied not by their own engines but by electric locomotives, the famous mules, which run on toothed rack rails atop the lock walls and hold the hull centred with cables.

The gates themselves are the most theatrical part of the machinery. The originals are riveted steel leaves up to eighty-two feet high and seven feet thick, yet each is a hollow, buoyant box, so well balanced on its hinges that a modest motor can swing hundreds of tonnes smoothly shut. Many of the 1914 gates are still in service. In 2016 the canal added a third lane with far larger chambers, rolling gates that slide sideways into recesses, and terraced water-saving basins that reclaim about three-fifths of each lockage. The scale changed; the idea did not. A century on, the canal still lifts ships across a continent using concrete boxes, rainwater, and gravity.
