The waiting room at King's Cross Station was nearly empty on this gray Thursday afternoon. Margaret sat on the wooden bench, watching the clock. When Thomas rushed through the door, breathless, she didn't look up.

"You're late," she said flatly.

"I know, I'm sorry. The taxi driver took a wrong turn and I couldn't—" He stopped abruptly, noticing the suitcase at her feet.

"What's that?"

Margaret's hands trembled slightly as she gripped her handbag. "I'm leaving, Thomas. I've decided I can't do this anymore."

His face went pale. "You can't be serious. What about us? What about our plans to visit Switzerland?" He sank onto the bench beside her. "Margaret, please listen to what I have to say. I spoke to my brother yesterday, and he's agreed to help us with the deposit. We can finally afford to—"

"No." Her voice was quiet but firm. "I ran into your ex-wife at the market last week. She told me things that..." Margaret paused, choosing her words carefully. "She said you'd made identical promises to her. The same promises about Switzerland, about a fresh start, about leaving your father's business."

Thomas looked stricken. "That's different. She misunderstood my intentions. I wasn't—"

A young woman with a newspaper interrupted from across the waiting room, muttering to herself: "Can't believe the price of rail tickets these days. Absolutely ridiculous."

"Exactly," Margaret said, gesturing toward the woman as if she'd proven a point. "Even strangers see how things are in this world. I spent three years making excuses for you—telling your mother we'd marry 'next spring,' telling my own family that you needed time to sort yourself out."

Thomas reached for her hand, but she pulled away. "My sister warned me about you. She said, 'Margaret, if he truly loved you, he wouldn't be seen with that accountant woman from his office.' And I defended you. Can you believe that?"

"That was nothing—purely business. I can explain everything."

"I'm sure you can." Margaret stood, lifting her suitcase. "My train leaves in twenty minutes. I'm going to Bath to stay with my aunt. She's offered me work at her dress shop." (She'd always been kind, unlike some members of her family.) "I have a life to build, Thomas, and it won't be waiting for you anymore."

He stood desperately. "When will you come back?"

"I won't," Margaret said, walking toward the platform. "Goodbye, Thomas."
