Before the container, loading a ship was a craft performed by hand. Cargo arrived at the quay as barrels, sacks, crates and bales of every conceivable size, and gangs of dockers stowed each item individually, fitting the pieces together like a puzzle so that nothing shifted at sea. A ship could spend as long in port as it spent crossing the ocean, and port charges often exceeded the cost of the voyage itself. Breakage and theft were routine, and the work was dangerous.

The decisive figure was Malcom McLean, an American trucking operator who had no background in shipping at all. His interest was in eliminating the handling step between lorry and vessel. In April 1956 he loaded fifty-eight truck bodies onto a converted tanker, the Ideal X, and sailed it from Newark to Houston. The economics were immediately obvious: the cost of loading fell from several dollars a ton to a few cents.

McLean was not the first to try the idea. Railways had carried standard boxes since the nineteenth century, and the United States Army had used a small container, the Conex box, in Korea. What McLean added was a system rather than a box. He designed a corner casting and a twist lock that allowed containers to be stacked, locked together and lifted by crane in one motion, and he then made the patent freely available so the design could become an industry standard.

Standardisation was the slow part. Through the late 1950s and 1960s, shipping lines, railways and national bodies argued over dimensions, because every operator preferred whichever size matched its existing equipment. The International Organization for Standardization eventually settled on lengths of twenty and forty feet, with a common width and agreed corner fittings, and by 1970 the essential standards were in place. Only then could a container packed in one country be lifted onto any ship, chassis or railcar in another.

The Vietnam War accelerated adoption. The United States military faced an enormous supply problem and containerisation solved it, which gave McLean guaranteed revenue and, on the return legs, a chance to build trade with Japan.

The consequences went far beyond the docks. Freight costs collapsed, which made it economic to manufacture components in one country and assemble them in another, and long global supply chains became possible. Old ports built around warehouses and manual labour, including much of the London docks, were abandoned for deepwater sites with cranes and space for stacking, such as Felixstowe and Rotterdam. Dock labour forces shrank by enormous margins, with bitter industrial disputes as a result. The plain steel box, unremarkable to look at, reshaped world trade more thoroughly than most celebrated inventions of the same century.
