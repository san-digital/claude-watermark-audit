The problem of finding longitude at sea is, at bottom, a problem of keeping time. Latitude had never been especially difficult. The height of the noon sun above the horizon, or the altitude of Polaris at night, gives a navigator his distance north or south of the equator with reasonable accuracy, and sailors had been doing it with quadrants and cross-staffs since the fifteenth century. Longitude offers no such natural marker. The earth turns through three hundred and sixty degrees in twenty-four hours, which is fifteen degrees of longitude every hour, so if a navigator knows the local time aboard his ship, established easily enough by observing the moment the sun reaches its highest point, and simultaneously knows the time at some fixed reference meridian, the difference between the two converts directly into his distance east or west. Four minutes of time is one degree of arc. The whole difficulty of two centuries lay in that second quantity: how to know, in the middle of an ocean, what o'clock it was somewhere else.

Without it, navigators relied on dead reckoning. A ship's position was estimated by taking the last known fix and adding the courses steered and the distances run, the latter measured by heaving a log line over the stern and counting knots as the sand ran through a glass. Errors compounded relentlessly. Currents were unknown and unmeasured, leeway was guessed, compass variation was poorly charted, and helmsmen steered imperfectly. Over a long Atlantic passage a landfall might be out by a hundred miles or more, which is why the standard practice was to sail to the latitude of the destination well out to sea and then run along that parallel until land appeared, a technique that was safe in principle, wasteful of time, and predictable enough to be exploited by pirates and privateers who knew exactly where to wait.

The consequences were not merely commercial. In October 1707 a returning squadron under Sir Cloudesley Shovell, believing itself safely west of the entrance to the English Channel, struck the rocks of the Isles of Scilly at night. Four ships were lost and something close to two thousand men died. The catastrophe was widely blamed on faulty navigation, and although historians have since argued that latitude error and poor charts played as large a part as any longitude failure, the disaster hardened political will. In 1714 Parliament passed the Longitude Act, which established a Board of Longitude and offered a graduated set of rewards, the greatest being twenty thousand pounds for a method capable of determining longitude to within half a degree after a voyage to the West Indies. That is thirty nautical miles at the equator, and it implies a timekeeping error of no more than about two minutes over six weeks at sea. Britain was not first in this. Philip III of Spain had offered a prize in 1598, and the Dutch States General had approached Galileo with an offer of their own.

Galileo had a genuine proposal. Having discovered the four large moons of Jupiter in 1610, he realised that their eclipses and occultations constitute a clock visible from anywhere on the night side of the earth. Tabulate the predicted times of those events at a reference meridian, observe the same event from a ship, and the time difference falls out. The method worked beautifully in principle and was, in fact, transformative on land. Giovanni Domenico Cassini published usable tables, and surveyors employing them redrew the map of France so drastically, pulling the Atlantic coast eastwards, that Louis XIV is supposed to have complained that his astronomers had cost him more territory than his enemies. At sea it was hopeless. Observing Jupiter's satellites requires a telescope of substantial magnification held steady on a rolling deck, which nobody managed, and it requires clear skies at precisely the predicted moment.

That left two serious contenders through the eighteenth century, and they were pursued in parallel and often in rivalry. The first was the method of lunar distances. The moon moves against the background of fixed stars at roughly its own diameter every hour, so the angular distance between the moon and a selected star or the sun functions as a celestial clock face. Johannes Werner had proposed the idea as early as 1514, but making it practical demanded three things that did not yet exist: a theory of lunar motion accurate enough to predict positions well in advance, tables computed from that theory and published in advance, and an instrument capable of measuring an angle to about a minute of arc from a moving deck. All three arrived in the middle of the century. John Hadley's reflecting octant of 1731, invented independently by Thomas Godfrey in Philadelphia, used a double-reflection principle that made the measured angle immune to the ship's motion, and John Campbell's extension of the arc produced the sextant in 1757. Tobias Mayer of Göttingen, building on Leonhard Euler's work, produced lunar tables of unprecedented accuracy in the 1750s. Nevil Maskelyne, who became Astronomer Royal in 1765, tested the method at sea, published The British Mariner's Guide in 1763, and from 1767 issued the annual Nautical Almanac, which pre-computed the lunar distances at three-hour intervals so that a navigator's work was reduced from a four-hour ordeal of spherical trigonometry to perhaps thirty minutes of tabular reduction. Lunars were cheap, needed no maintenance, and could not run down. They also demanded a competent observer, a clear sky, and a visible moon, which excludes several days each month.

The second contender was mechanical. Christiaan Huygens had built the first pendulum clock in 1656 and understood immediately what it might mean for navigation, but a pendulum is useless on a pitching ship and its rate changes with latitude because gravity does. A marine timekeeper had to survive motion in six directions, temperature swings from the Baltic to the tropics, changes in humidity and barometric pressure, salt air, and the thickening of oil in cold weather, and it had to do all this while accumulating less than a couple of seconds of error a day for months on end. Almost nobody believed it possible. Isaac Newton had told the Board as much.

John Harrison, a self-taught Lincolnshire carpenter and clockmaker, spent his working life proving otherwise. His first sea clock, H1, completed around 1735, was a brass machine of some thirty-four kilograms using linked balances that counteracted each other so that the motion of the ship affected both equally, and gridiron pendulum principles adapted to compensate for temperature. Trialled on a voyage to Lisbon in 1736, it performed well enough on the return leg to secure further funding. H2 followed and was set aside when Harrison identified a flaw. H3 occupied him for seventeen years and introduced the bimetallic strip and the caged roller bearing, both of which outlived it. Then, remarkably, Harrison abandoned the whole approach. Watch mechanisms, he had come to realise, oscillate fast enough that a ship's motion disturbs them less, and a small high-frequency balance with a strong spring is inherently more stable than a large slow one. H4, finished in 1759, is a watch about thirteen centimetres across, in a silver pair case, and it looks like an ordinary if handsome pocket watch of its period.

On its trial voyage to Jamaica in 1761 and 1762, carried by Harrison's son William aboard the Deptford, H4 was found on arrival to have lost around five seconds in eighty-one days, an error corresponding to roughly one nautical mile of longitude. The Board, unpersuaded that a single result proved reliability rather than luck, demanded a second trial, which took place to Barbados in 1764 and produced a comparably excellent result. What followed was a protracted and bitter dispute. The Board, with Maskelyne among its members and a professional commitment to lunar distances, required Harrison to disclose his mechanism completely, to surrender his timekeepers, and to demonstrate that others could reproduce the work. He received part of the money, fought for the rest, and only obtained further payment in 1773 after petitioning Parliament directly with the sympathy of George III, and even then never formally won the prize.

The vindication came from copies. Larcum Kendall produced K1, a faithful reproduction of H4, which James Cook carried on his second voyage between 1772 and 1775 and praised without reservation, charting the Pacific with an accuracy that had simply not been available before. But H4 was a handmade masterpiece, ruinously expensive and slow to build, and the practical revolution required a chronometer that a workshop could turn out in numbers. John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw supplied it in the 1780s and 1790s, developing the spring detent escapement and a compensation balance with bimetallic arms carrying adjustable weights, a design so sound that it remained essentially unchanged for a hundred and fifty years. Prices fell from hundreds of pounds to a few tens. By the early nineteenth century the Royal Navy was issuing chronometers as standard equipment, and by the time HMS Beagle sailed in 1831 with Robert FitzRoy she carried twenty-two of them, the redundancy allowing errors in any one to be detected by comparison.

Even then the two methods coexisted for decades. Prudent navigators used lunars to check the rate of their chronometers on long passages, and the Nautical Almanac continued publishing lunar distance tables until 1907. What finally made the chronometer wholly self-sufficient was the distribution of accurate time itself: the time ball dropped at Greenwich from 1833 so that ships in the Thames could set their watches, the electric telegraph, and eventually wireless time signals broadcast from shore stations in the years before the First World War, which allowed a navigator to correct his instrument at sea whenever he wished. The International Meridian Conference of 1884 settled the last piece of the puzzle by adopting Greenwich as the prime meridian, so that the reference time everyone had been struggling to carry across the oceans finally had a single agreed home.
