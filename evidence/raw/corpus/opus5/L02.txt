I want to be clear at the outset that I was not starving before this. I had food. I ate it. What I did not have, at thirty-two years old, was any relationship with cooking that a reasonable person would describe as a relationship. It was more of an arrangement. A hostage situation with occasional pasta.

My repertoire, if you can call it that, consisted of four things. Pasta with a jar of something. Stir fry, meaning whatever vegetables were dying in the drawer plus soy sauce plus a quantity of oil that I now understand to have been genuinely alarming. Eggs, prepared in the manner of a man who has never once let a pan get hot before adding the eggs. And a thing I called chilli, which I have since learned was actually just mince. Seasoned mince. Mince with ambition.

The moment it changed was not inspiring. I would love to tell you I had a transcendent meal in a small restaurant somewhere and walked out changed. What actually happened is that I made a stir fry for someone I was trying to impress and she said, kindly, in the voice you use with a dog that has brought you a dead bird, "This is really nice." And then she ate about a third of it. And I looked down at my own plate, properly looked at it for the first time in about a decade, and I thought: this is grey. Why is this grey. Everything on this plate is grey.

So I decided to learn. And because I am the kind of person who cannot simply start doing a thing, I spent the first three weeks researching how to learn to cook rather than cooking. I read about knife skills. I watched forty videos about knife skills. I bought a knife. It was, everyone agreed, an excellent knife. I then did not use it for a fortnight because I had also read that you should never put a good knife in the dishwasher and I had not yet emotionally processed what that would mean for my life.

Here is the first genuinely useful thing I learned, and it is embarrassing how late it came: heat is an ingredient. I had spent my entire adult life cooking everything on medium. Everything. Steak, onions, eggs, garlic, all of it, medium, the temperature of a man who does not want to cause trouble. Turns out the reason my food had no flavour is that flavour largely comes from things going brown, and things do not go brown at medium. They go beige. They sweat, sadly, in their own liquid, and then you eat sad wet beige food and wonder why restaurants taste different.

The second thing, and this one took longer, is salt. I was salting food at the table like a Victorian. Salt at the end is a crust on the outside of a bland thing. Salt during is seasoning. I salted a pot of pasta water properly for the first time and honestly felt like I'd been let in on a conspiracy. Everyone knew. Nobody told me. My mother knew and did not tell me, and I've raised it with her, and she says she did tell me, repeatedly, throughout my entire childhood, which I think is a matter of interpretation.

Digression: I have become insufferable about ingredients, and I'd like to apologise to everyone in advance. It happened so fast. One month I'm buying pre-grated cheese in a bag, the next I am standing in a shop holding two different tins of tomatoes and thinking about them. Genuinely thinking about them. Comparatively. I could feel it happening and I could not stop it. My friend Dan came round and I said the words "I only really use anchovies for depth" out loud, in my own kitchen, in front of a witness, and he just looked at me for a really long time.

The failures deserve their own section because they were spectacular and I think people gloss over them. I made a risotto so aggressively wrong that it achieved a texture I can only describe as architectural. I attempted a béchamel and produced wallpaper paste with a hint of nutmeg. I once tried to flip something in a pan the way they do on television and redistributed approximately half of it onto the hob, where it burned instantly, filling the flat with smoke and triggering the alarm, which brought my downstairs neighbour up in a dressing gown at ten past nine on a Tuesday. She was very gracious. I gave her the half that stayed in the pan. It was fine. It was actually quite good, which was somehow worse.

I burned garlic constantly for about six months. Garlic goes from raw to perfect to bitter and ruined in roughly eleven seconds, and there is no warning, and you cannot rescue it, and if you have burned the garlic you have to start again, and I refused to accept this for so long that I served several people meals that tasted faintly of ash and stubbornness.

But the thing nobody tells you, the actual reason I'd recommend doing this to anybody, is that cooking properly restructures your week. Not in a wellness way. I'm not about to tell you it's meditative, because half the time it is the opposite of meditative, it is me swearing at a colander. It's that you stop making a hundred small joyless decisions. You know what's in the fridge because you put it there on purpose. You stop staring into a food delivery app at half seven with the particular hollow feeling of someone about to spend eighteen pounds on something they won't enjoy. You start having people over, because feeding people is a much lower-stakes way of seeing them than "shall we get a drink", which for some reason always requires three weeks of scheduling.

I make bread now. I'm not going to talk about the bread at length because people who talk about their bread are the worst, but I will say this: I understand them. I understand the bread people now. I have been to the other side and I have seen what they see.

Where I've landed, two years in, is that I'm not a good cook. I want to be honest about that because I think the internet is full of people who learned to cook and then immediately started presenting themselves as an authority. I'm a competent cook with about fifteen dishes I can genuinely nail and a broad enough understanding that I can improvise without producing a war crime. I still can't do fish properly. My pastry is a catastrophe every single time and I've stopped pretending it's a learning journey.

But my food isn't grey anymore. And when someone eats what I've made and says "this is really nice," they now eat the whole thing, which is how you know.

Anyway. Salt your pasta water. That's the post. Everything above this sentence was optional.
