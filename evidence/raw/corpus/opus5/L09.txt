The saleroom at Hallow Bridge had been a corn exchange, then a cinema, then nothing for eleven years, and it still smelled faintly of all three. Ray Dunnock stood at the rostrum with his glasses pushed up into hair he no longer really had, going through the book while Bev set out lot 214, which was a stuffed pike in a case with a crack across the glass.

"Don't put it at the front," he said. "It frightens people."

"It's got a reserve on it."

"It's got a face on it, Bev, that's the trouble." He licked his thumb and turned a page. "Middle of the third row. Between the copper and the thing with the legs."

Ginny Pike came in at half past nine with her coat still buttoned and a paper cup she'd clearly bought somewhere better than here. She'd been coming to Hallow Bridge for twenty-two years and had a particular way of walking the lots, slowly, hands behind her back, like an inspecting officer who has already decided somebody is in trouble.

"Ray."

"Ginny."

"Where'd you get the Sunderland lustre?"

"Which one?"

"Don't." She didn't look up from the table. "Lot 88. The jug with the frog in it."

Ray sighed and came down off the rostrum, which he did roughly four times a year. "House clearance out past Welbourne. Old girl died in March, ninety-six, sharp as anything right up until she wasn't. Grandson's here somewhere, he's" — he scanned the room — "he's the one by the door pretending he isn't crying."

"He's about twelve."

"He's twenty-six and he's had a rough spring, so be nice."

"I'm always nice," Ginny said, which was not true and which both of them knew was not true, and she went over to the boy anyway.

Tom Ferriday had been standing by the door for twenty minutes trying to work out whether he was allowed to sit down. Everything of his grandmother's was in this room, in rows, with numbers stuck to it, and the effect was roughly what he'd expected and completely unlike it.

"You're the Welbourne house," Ginny said. Not a question.

"Sorry — yes. Tom."

"Ginny Pike. I deal in ceramics, mainly north-east, some Staffordshire." She held out a hand and he shook it, slightly too hard. "I'm going to bid on your grandmother's lustre jug and I'd rather tell you that now than have you find out at half eleven and think I'm circling."

"Right." He blinked. "Is that — do people normally say that?"

"No." She drank some of her coffee. "But you look like you're about to watch a house get taken apart, which you are, and I've found it's easier if you know who some of the vultures are." A pause. "Did you know it was good? The jug."

"I didn't know any of it was anything. She used it for pens."

Ginny made a small noise that could have been amusement or grief. "Of course she did."

Bev appeared at Tom's elbow with a clipboard and the particular briskness of a woman who has run this room for nine years and is not paid enough. "You're Ferriday, yes? I've got you down for the whole hundred and forty through to two-oh-nine, plus the two boxes of linen we took in late. If anything doesn't sell it comes back to you or we can put it in the general sale next month for a fiver a lot, your call, don't decide now."

"Two boxes of — sorry, there were three."

"There were three. One of them was moths." She said it without cruelty. "I've got the good tablecloth out separately, the big damask one, it's in with the pike."

"The what?"

"You'll see," said Ray, from behind them.

At five to ten the room filled the way these rooms do, all at once and mostly with men in fleeces. Ray climbed back up, tapped the microphone twice — it howled, it always howled, nobody had ever fixed it — and said the things he said every fortnight.

"Right, ladies and gentlemen, morning. Usual conditions, buyer's premium is twenty per cent plus VAT on the premium, cash or card, nothing leaves the room 'til it's paid for. If you're bidding and I don't see you, wave properly, don't do the thing with the eyebrow, Derek, you know who you are. Lot one."

It went quickly. Ray had a rhythm that sounded careless and wasn't; he'd been doing it since 1989 and could sell a box of assorted spanners in eleven seconds flat.

Lot 88 came up at twenty past ten.

"Sunderland lustre jug, transfer print, frog to the interior, bit of wear round the rim. Twenty pounds for it. Twenty. Twenty anywhere, thank you madam, twenty-five, thirty, thirty-five" — the number climbed past a hundred and the room, which had been talking, stopped talking — "hundred and eighty, two hundred, two-twenty against you sir, two-forty" and Ginny, who had not appeared to move at all, took it at four hundred and sixty pounds.

Tom said, out loud, to nobody, "It had pens in it."

The man next to him nodded sympathetically. "They always do."

Afterwards, in the yard, while Bev worked through the paying-out and a queue of people carried furniture past them at knee height, Ginny found him again.

"Four-sixty's a fair price," she said. "I'll get seven for it, maybe eight, if I'm patient, and I'm patient. I'm telling you that as well."

"You don't have to keep — "

"I know I don't." She lit a cigarette, which she had cut down to four a day and this was the second. "Can I tell you something useless? My mother had a shop in Bishop Auckland. When she died I sold the lot in a fortnight because I couldn't stand to look at it, and I've spent thirty years buying bits of it back off other dealers without meaning to. Every so often something comes across a table and I think, ah, there you are." She tapped ash carefully into a bucket of sand. "It doesn't go anywhere, is what I'm saying. It just changes hands."

Tom looked at the loading bay, where two men were carrying his grandmother's wardrobe, the one he had hidden inside during exactly one game of hide and seek in 1998, out towards a transit van.

"Ray said something similar," he said. "He said, 'It's not a funeral, it's just a slower one.' Which I thought was a horrible thing to say to somebody."

"That does sound like Ray."

"He was right, though."

"He usually is. It's his single worst quality." She dropped the cigarette into the sand. "Come and find me at the next Welbourne sale. I'll show you what to look for, and then next time you'll know the jug's a jug before you put pens in it."

Inside, Ray was three lots from the end and losing the room.

"Lot two-fourteen, and I'll be honest with you, it's a pike in a case, the glass has gone across the top and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Somebody caught this in — " he peered at the label — "1911, and somebody's had it on a wall ever since, which tells you everything about the English. Ten pounds. Ten. Five, then. Five pounds, a fish, come on."

Bev, from the floor, without looking up: "Two pound fifty."

"Sold, to my own staff, which is against every rule I've got." He banged the gavel. "You're taking it home, Bev."

"I'm putting it in your office."

"You're taking it home."

The pike, whose expression did not change, was carried out under her arm at eleven minutes past twelve, past the wardrobe, past the boy by the door, out into a car park where it had begun, very lightly, to rain.
