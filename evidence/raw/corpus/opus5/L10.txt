For nearly three hundred years, mariners could tell with reasonable confidence how far north or south they were and had almost no reliable way of knowing how far east or west. The asymmetry has a straightforward physical cause. Latitude leaves visible marks on the sky: the elevation of the pole star above the horizon corresponds directly to your latitude in the northern hemisphere, and the altitude of the sun at local noon, corrected using published tables of solar declination, gives the same answer anywhere. A navigator with a quadrant, a backstaff or later a sextant, and a printed almanac, could establish his latitude to within a few miles. Longitude has no such signature. Every meridian looks identical from the deck of a ship, and the earth's rotation carries the whole celestial sphere overhead in a way that makes one line of longitude indistinguishable from another unless you have some independent reference.

That reference is time. Because the planet turns once in twenty-four hours, fifteen degrees of longitude corresponds exactly to one hour, one degree to four minutes, and one minute of arc to four seconds. A navigator who knows the time aboard his own ship, obtained by observing local apparent noon, and who simultaneously knows the time at some agreed home meridian, can subtract one from the other and convert the difference straight into an east or west displacement. The entire longitude problem reduces to the question of how to carry the home meridian's time across an ocean, or how to read it off the sky.

In the absence of a solution, ships navigated by dead reckoning, which is estimation dressed up with instruments. From a known departure point, the navigator plotted the courses steered by compass and the distances run, the latter measured by streaming a log line from the stern and counting the knots that passed through his fingers as a sandglass emptied. To this he applied corrections for magnetic variation, for leeway when sailing close to the wind, and for whatever he believed the currents were doing. Every one of those corrections was approximate, and their errors accumulated over a passage rather than cancelling. Landfall after five weeks at sea might be a hundred miles out. The standard defence was latitude sailing: run to the latitude of your destination well offshore, then hold that parallel and sail down it until land appears. It worked, at the cost of extra days at sea, extra scurvy, and complete predictability, since privateers knew perfectly well which parallels the treasure ships would be following.

Occasionally the failure was catastrophic. In October 1707 a British fleet returning from Toulon under Sir Cloudesley Shovell ran onto the Isles of Scilly in fog and darkness, losing four ships and around two thousand men, including the admiral. The disaster was popularly attributed to an inability to fix position, and it fed a growing public conviction that the state should intervene. In 1714 Parliament passed the Longitude Act, establishing a Board of Longitude and offering rewards on a sliding scale: ten thousand pounds for a method accurate to one degree at the end of a voyage to the West Indies, fifteen thousand for two thirds of a degree, and twenty thousand for half a degree. That top figure demanded that a method be good to thirty nautical miles after six weeks at sea, equivalent to a timekeeping error of about two minutes. It was an extraordinary sum, and an extraordinary specification. Britain was not alone in trying; Spain had offered a prize as early as 1598, and the Dutch had made overtures to Galileo.

Galileo's own proposal was ingenious and, on land, decisive. In 1610 he had observed the four large satellites of Jupiter, and he realised that their eclipses behind the planet occur at instants that are the same for every observer on earth. Tabulate the predicted times against a home meridian, observe the actual event from wherever you are, and the difference gives longitude. Giovanni Domenico Cassini refined the tables, and expeditions using the method resurveyed France so thoroughly, moving the western coastline substantially eastward, that Louis XIV was said to have lost more territory to his own academicians than to any enemy. At sea it was hopeless. Resolving Jupiter's moons requires a telescope of real magnification, and holding one steady on a rolling deck defeated every attempt, including Galileo's own binocular helmet contraption.

Two workable approaches emerged in the eighteenth century, and their advocates spent decades in opposition. The first, lunar distances, treats the moon as the hand of a clock. The moon travels eastward against the fixed stars at roughly half a degree an hour, so the measured angular separation between the moon's limb and a chosen star or the sun encodes Greenwich time. Johannes Werner had suggested this in 1514, but three prerequisites were missing. An accurate theory of lunar motion arrived through Leonhard Euler's analysis and Tobias Mayer's tables of the 1750s. A publication schedule arrived when Nevil Maskelyne, appointed Astronomer Royal in 1765, launched the annual Nautical Almanac in 1767, pre-computing lunar distances at three-hour intervals so that a navigator's calculation dropped from around four hours of spherical trigonometry to perhaps half an hour of tabular reduction. And the instrument arrived with John Hadley's reflecting octant of 1731, invented independently by Thomas Godfrey in Philadelphia, whose double-reflection principle held the image of one object superimposed on another regardless of the ship's motion, extended by John Campbell in 1757 into the sextant.

The second approach was to build a clock that could go to sea. Christiaan Huygens had produced the pendulum clock in 1656 and immediately imagined the application, but a pendulum's rate depends on local gravity and it cannot survive a ship's motion at all. A marine timekeeper would have to endure pitch, roll, temperature ranging from the Arctic to the tropics, humidity, salt, changing barometric pressure and the thickening of lubricants in cold weather, and still lose or gain no more than a second or two a day for months. Newton had told the Board that this was the least promising line of enquiry available.

John Harrison, a Lincolnshire joiner turned self-taught clockmaker, disagreed for the rest of his life. His first sea clock, H1, completed around 1735, weighed some thirty-four kilograms and used two linked bar balances so arranged that a movement affecting one affected the other equally and cancelled out; it used no lubrication in its critical parts, since Harrison had already worked out how to make wooden wheels of lignum vitae that oiled themselves. Its trial to Lisbon in 1736 was encouraging enough to secure funding. H2 was abandoned when Harrison spotted a theoretical defect. H3 consumed seventeen years and yielded the bimetallic strip and the caged roller bearing, both still in use. Then he changed his mind entirely, having noticed that a small, fast-oscillating balance driven by a strong spring is inherently less disturbed by motion than a large slow one. H4, finished in 1759, is a silver pocket watch about thirteen centimetres in diameter.

Sent to Jamaica in 1761 in the care of Harrison's son William, H4 arrived after eighty-one days having lost about five seconds, an error worth roughly a nautical mile of longitude. The Board wanted a repeat, which took place to Barbados in 1764 with a similar outcome. What followed was less about instruments than about institutions. The Board, on which Maskelyne sat and which was professionally invested in lunar distances, demanded full disclosure of the mechanism, surrender of the timekeepers, and proof that other craftsmen could replicate the work. Harrison received part of the money, litigated and petitioned for years, and eventually secured further payment from Parliament in 1773 with the personal support of George III. He never formally received the prize itself, and died three years later.

The practical vindication came from copies and successors. Larcum Kendall's K1, a close reproduction of H4, sailed with James Cook on his second Pacific voyage from 1772 to 1775 and allowed charting of an accuracy previously unavailable. But H4 was a single handmade object, and the revolution required affordable instruments made in quantity. John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw supplied them through the 1780s and 1790s, developing the spring detent escapement and a bimetallic compensation balance with adjustable weights, a configuration so effective that it survived essentially unaltered into the twentieth century. Prices fell by an order of magnitude, the Royal Navy began issuing chronometers as standard, and by 1831 HMS Beagle carried twenty-two of them so that any one instrument's drift could be caught by comparison with the rest.

Lunars and chronometers coexisted for a century, the former used to check the latter's rate on long passages, and the Nautical Almanac carried lunar distance tables until 1907. What finally retired the method was the distribution of time itself: the Greenwich time ball, dropped daily from 1833 for ships in the Thames; the electric telegraph, which allowed longitudes ashore to be determined by direct comparison; and wireless time signals from the early twentieth century, which let a navigator reset his chronometer mid-ocean. The International Meridian Conference of 1884, in adopting Greenwich as the prime meridian, gave the reference time a single agreed home, and the problem that had shipwrecked fleets became a matter of listening to a radio.
