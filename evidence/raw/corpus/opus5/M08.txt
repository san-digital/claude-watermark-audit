Vienna and Prague sit roughly three hundred kilometres apart, and for two centuries they formed the two poles of a single musical culture, one imperial and cosmopolitan, the other increasingly national and defiant.

Vienna's claim rests on an extraordinary concentration of talent. Haydn spent decades in the service of the Esterházy family before his late London years; Mozart arrived from Salzburg and died in the city at thirty-five; Beethoven came from Bonn and never left. Schubert, the only true native of the group, wrote over six hundred Lieder in a short life spent largely on the city's margins. Later came Brahms, who settled there in 1862, and Bruckner, who arrived from Sankt Florian with his organ improvisations and his enormous symphonies. Johann Strauß the younger supplied the waltzes that gave the city its lighter reputation, though the Ländler underneath them is a peasant dance, and Mahler took exactly that folk material and made it strange.

The break with tonality also happened in Vienna. Arnold Schönberg, together with his pupils Alban Berg and Anton Webern, formed what is still called the Second Viennese School, moving from the saturated chromaticism of Verklärte Nacht to free atonality and then to the twelve-tone method. Their audiences were frequently hostile; the Skandalkonzert of 1913 ended in a brawl.

Prague's story runs in parallel but with a different accent. The city adored Mozart, whose Don Giovanni had its premiere at the Estates Theatre in 1787, and he is said to have remarked that his Praguers understood him. The nineteenth century then turned music into a vehicle for Czech national identity. Bedřich Smetana wrote The Bartered Bride and the cycle Má vlast, whose second movement, Vltava, traces the river from its sources to Prague. Antonín Dvořák followed, drawing on Bohemian dance forms in the Slavonic Dances and carrying that approach to America, where the New World symphony was written. His son-in-law Josef Suk continued the line.

Leoš Janáček, working mainly in Brno rather than Prague, developed a wholly individual style from what he called speech melody, notating the rhythms and contours of overheard Czech conversation and building vocal lines from them. His late operas, and the Glagolitic Mass, sound like nothing else.

The vocabulary of this world remains largely German and Italian. A Kapellmeister directed the chapel; a Singspiel mixed song with spoken dialogue; a Ständchen is a serenade. French terms entered through the salon, so Gabriel Fauré, César Franck and Camille Saint-Saëns were known and played, and Debussy's harmonic freedom reached both cities before 1914. The First World War ended the empire that had held this musical world together, but the repertoire it produced still fills the Musikverein and the Rudolfinum every season.
