The waiting room at Harlow Mill smelled of wet wool and the paraffin heater nobody had thought to turn off since March. Three of them sat on the bench: the old man with the dog, the woman in the good coat, and a boy of about nineteen who had been staring at the departure board through the window for eleven minutes.

"It's not coming," the old man said.

The woman didn't look up from her book. "It says nineteen minutes past."

"It's said nineteen minutes past for half an hour. That board's a work of fiction." He shifted, and the dog resettled itself across his boots with a sigh. "My brother worked signals at Bishop's Stortford for twenty-two years. He used to say, and I quote him exactly, 'The timetable is a promise, not a prediction.' Made a lot more sense once I'd stopped commuting."

The boy turned round. "There's a replacement bus. It's on the app."

"There's always a replacement bus on the app," said the woman, and she folded the corner of her page down, which the old man visibly disapproved of. "Whether it exists in the physical world is a separate question."

"No, it's — look, it's got a number plate and everything."

"So did the one I waited for at Audley End."

The old man laughed, a short dry sound. The dog's tail moved twice against the tiles. "She's got you there, son."

The boy came and sat down, two spaces along, and put his phone face down on his knee, which for his generation is roughly the equivalent of taking off a hat. "I'm supposed to be at my sister's by seven. It's her — well. It's a thing. She'll be fine about it, she'll just do the voice."

"The voice," the woman repeated.

"You know the voice. 'No, honestly, it's fine.'" He did it rather well, a bright flat cheerfulness with something underneath.

"I know the voice," she said. "I invented it."

Rain came on harder against the window. The heater ticked. Somewhere down the line a freight went through without slowing, and all three of them watched it go, and the dog didn't move at all.

"Right," said the old man, hauling himself up with both hands on the bench edge. "There's a pub two hundred yards that way that does a decent pie, and if the train comes we'll hear it from the garden."

The woman closed her book properly this time. "And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll have had the pie."

The boy looked at his phone, then at the two of them already halfway to the door, and made the calculation that people of nineteen make roughly once a year and remember for decades.

"Wait," he said. "Wait, I'm coming."
