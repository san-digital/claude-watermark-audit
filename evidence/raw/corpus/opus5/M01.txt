The idea of cutting a waterway across the Isthmus of Panama was discussed from the sixteenth century onwards, but the first serious attempt began in 1881 under Ferdinand de Lesseps, whose triumph at Suez had persuaded him that a sea-level channel could be dug through Panama as well. Suez, however, crossed flat desert. Panama offered dense rainforest, torrential rainfall, the unstable rock of the Culebra Cut, and the Chagres River, which could rise several metres in a single day. Yellow fever and malaria killed labourers in enormous numbers. The French company collapsed in 1889 having moved a great quantity of earth and lost roughly twenty thousand lives.

When the United States took the project over in 1904, its engineers accepted what de Lesseps had refused to accept: a sea-level cut was impractical, so ships would have to be lifted over the isthmus and lowered down the other side. John Frank Stevens argued for damming the Chagres at Gatun, creating an artificial lake about twenty-six metres above sea level. This both tamed the destructive river and supplied a ready-made navigation channel across most of the crossing. George Washington Goethals carried the scheme through to completion, while William Gorgas ran a sanitation campaign against the mosquito that cut the death rate dramatically. The canal opened in August 1914.

The lifting is done by three sets of locks: Gatun, with three steps, on the Atlantic side, then Pedro Miguel with one step and Miraflores with two on the Pacific side. Each of the original chambers measures 33.5 metres wide and about 320 metres long, dimensions that defined the Panamax class of ship for a century. The locks operate entirely by gravity, with no pumps of any kind. Water runs from Gatun Lake into culverts roughly five and a half metres in diameter, buried in the lock walls, then through cross culverts and up through many small openings in the chamber floor, so the chamber fills evenly rather than in a surge that would throw a ship about. A lockage takes eight to ten minutes to fill or drain, and emptying simply lets the water fall to the next chamber down or out to sea.

Vessels are held in position by electric locomotives, known as mules, running on rack rails along the lock walls. They steady and centre the ship rather than tow it, since most ships move under their own power. The miter gates are hollow steel leaves that float, and they close at an angle pointing upstream so that the pressure of the higher water forces them more tightly together.

Every transit consumes a large volume of fresh water, all of it supplied by rainfall collecting in Gatun Lake, which makes drought a genuine constraint on traffic. The expansion completed in 2016 added a third and wider lane using sliding rolling gates instead of miter gates, together with water-saving basins that recover a substantial share of each lockage.
