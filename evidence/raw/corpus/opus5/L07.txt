By the middle of the nineteenth century London had a transport problem that no city had faced before, because no city had ever been that large. The population had passed two million and the main line railway companies, forbidden by Parliament from driving their tracks into the historic centre, terminated in a ring of great stations around the edge: Paddington, Euston, King's Cross, and later St Pancras. Everyone arriving from the north and west then had to cross the last two or three miles by road, and the roads were choked with omnibuses, hansom cabs, carts and livestock. Charles Pearson, Solicitor to the City of London, spent two decades arguing that the answer was to put a railway under the streets. He was widely regarded as an eccentric and he was proved right.

The Metropolitan Railway opened on 10 January 1863, running three and three quarter miles from Paddington to Farringdon Street. It was the first underground passenger railway anywhere. The construction method, cut and cover, was brutally simple: dig an enormous trench along the line of an existing road, build brick retaining walls and a roof, then put the road back on top. Whole streets disappeared for months, sewers had to be diverted, and in 1862 the Fleet sewer burst into the works and flooded them. Roughly forty thousand people travelled on the first day, and passenger numbers in the first year exceeded nine million.

The experience was not pleasant. Trains were hauled by steam locomotives fitted with condensing apparatus intended to divert exhaust steam into water tanks rather than into the tunnel, but they still produced smoke and sulphurous fumes, and the ventilation shafts and open cuttings never coped. Carriages were lit by gas carried in bags on the roof. Contemporary accounts describe an atmosphere that passengers were assured, by the company, was actually beneficial to asthma sufferers. Nobody believed this, and it was not true.

Expansion continued regardless. The Metropolitan District Railway, a separate and frequently hostile company, built the southern side of what became the Inner Circle, and after years of mutual obstruction the circle was finally completed in 1884. The two companies shared the tracks and detested one another. Meanwhile the Metropolitan pushed far out into Buckinghamshire, buying land as it went and developing housing estates alongside the line, an area its own publicity department later christened Metro-land.

The next leap was technological. Cut and cover was cheap only where a wide road existed above; under the dense fabric of the City it was impossible. Peter Barlow and James Henry Greathead had demonstrated a tunnelling shield in building the Tower Subway under the Thames in 1870, and the technique allowed circular cast iron tunnels to be driven at depth through London clay without disturbing anything on the surface. The problem was traction, since steam was clearly unusable in a sealed deep tube. Electricity supplied the answer. The City and South London Railway opened in 1890 between King William Street and Stockwell, the first deep level electric tube railway in the world. Its carriages had tiny high windows on the theory that there was nothing to see, and passengers promptly named them padded cells.

The Central London Railway followed in 1900, running from Shepherd's Bush to Bank with a flat fare of twopence, which earned it the name the Twopenny Tube. Its success attracted capital, and specifically it attracted Charles Tyson Yerkes, an American financier with a shady record in Chicago transit. Between 1900 and 1902 Yerkes assembled the Underground Electric Railways Company of London, took over several struggling schemes and built a power station at Lots Road to supply them. Three new tubes opened in quick succession in 1906 and 1907: the Baker Street and Waterloo, immediately nicknamed the Bakerloo, the Great Northern Piccadilly and Brompton, and the Charing Cross Euston and Hampstead. Yerkes also funded the electrification of the older sub-surface lines, which finally ended steam operation on the Circle in 1905. He died in 1905 with the group deep in debt, and it was left to others to make it pay.

Those others were Albert Stanley, later Lord Ashfield, and Frank Pick. Stanley handled the commercial consolidation, absorbing bus and tram operators so that the whole network stopped competing with itself. Pick handled everything a passenger could see, and in doing so effectively invented modern corporate design. He commissioned Edward Johnston to draw a typeface for the system in 1916, a clean humanist sans serif still in use in a revised form today. He standardised the bar and circle roundel. He commissioned posters from serious artists and gave them unusual freedom. He supported Harry Beck, an engineering draughtsman who in 1931 sketched a map of the network in his own time that abandoned geography altogether, straightening every line to horizontal, vertical or forty five degrees and expanding the crowded centre relative to the sprawling suburbs. The management thought it too radical. A trial print run in 1933 sold out, and the diagram has since been copied by transit systems on every continent. Pick also brought in the architect Charles Holden, whose stations at Arnos Grove, Southgate and Sudbury Town brought European modernism to outer London suburbs in brick, concrete and glass.

In 1933 the whole tangle of private companies was brought together as the London Passenger Transport Board, a public corporation covering trains, buses, trams and coaches across a defined region.

The Second World War gave the system a role nobody had designed for. The authorities initially opposed the use of stations as air raid shelters, fearing that people would refuse to come back up, but Londoners simply bought platform tickets and stayed, and official policy gave way. At the peak around 170,000 people sheltered underground, with bunks, canteens and libraries organised in some stations. The protection was not absolute. Bombs penetrated at Balham and Bank with heavy loss of life, and the worst incident of all involved no bomb whatsoever: in March 1943 a crush on the stairs at Bethnal Green killed 173 people. Disused tunnels served other purposes. Part of the Central line east of Leytonstone became an aircraft component factory, the Cabinet War Rooms had an emergency link, and the closed Aldwych branch stored objects from the British Museum.

Post-war expansion was slower and more expensive. The Victoria line, opened in stages from 1968, was the first new tube across central London for over sixty years and introduced automatic train operation, with the driver responsible for closing the doors and pressing two start buttons. The Jubilee line followed in 1979, taking over the Bakerloo's Stanmore branch, and was extended east through Docklands to Stratford in 1999. That extension is architecturally the most ambitious thing the system has built, with cathedral-scale stations at Canary Wharf and Westminster and, for the first time in London, platform edge doors.

Safety changed the system twice. The fire at King's Cross in November 1987, which killed 31 people, began under a wooden escalator and developed with a violence that engineers had not previously understood, a phenomenon later named the trench effect. The subsequent inquiry led to the removal of wooden escalators, a total smoking ban, and a wholesale reform of management responsibility for safety. The suicide bombings of 7 July 2005, which struck three trains and a bus and killed 52 people, reshaped policing and surveillance across the network.

Ticketing moved from paper to the Oyster smartcard in 2003 and then to ordinary contactless bank cards from 2014, a change that quietly removed most of the queueing from the system. A limited Night Tube service began in 2016. The Elizabeth line, opened in 2022 after long delay, is not technically part of the Underground but is the largest addition to central London rail capacity in generations.

The oldest parts of the network remain the hardest to modernise. Deep tube tunnels were bored just large enough for the trains of the 1900s, which leaves almost no room for air conditioning equipment or for the air it would need to reject. Over a century the surrounding clay has warmed by several degrees, so the tunnels no longer act as a heat sink at all. Cooling the Victorian and Edwardian tubes is a problem the system has still not solved, and it is a direct inheritance from decisions taken by engineers who expected steam to be the worst of their difficulties.
