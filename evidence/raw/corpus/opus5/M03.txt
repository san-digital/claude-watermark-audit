A B-tree index speeds up lookups by turning a linear scan of a table into a short descent through a shallow, sorted tree. Without an index, finding rows where email = 'x' means reading every page of the heap: cost proportional to table size. With a B-tree, the database reads a handful of pages regardless of how large the table has grown.

The structure is a balanced multi-way search tree. Every node is one disk page, typically eight or sixteen kilobytes. Internal nodes hold sorted separator keys plus child pointers, and leaf nodes hold keys paired with row identifiers, or in a clustered index the row data itself. Because a page holds hundreds of keys rather than the two children of a binary tree, the fan-out is enormous. A fan-out of a few hundred means a three or four level tree indexes hundreds of millions of rows. That is the whole trick: the tree is wide and shallow, so the number of page reads per lookup is tiny and grows only logarithmically, with a very large logarithm base.

A point lookup walks from the root, binary searching within each node to choose the correct child, until it reaches a leaf. Three or four page accesses, and the upper levels are almost always cached in memory because every query touches them, so in practice you often pay for one physical read at the leaf. This is what a plan showing an index seek is describing.

The leaves are linked in key order, which is why the same structure serves range queries. Once you have located the first key at or above your lower bound, you simply walk the leaf chain until you pass the upper bound. That gives you BETWEEN, greater-than, less-than, prefix matches on strings, and ordered output without a sort step. It is also why a B-tree can satisfy ORDER BY for free and why a composite index on (a, b) helps queries filtering on a, or on a and b, but not queries filtering on b alone: sort order is defined left to right.

Balance is maintained on write. Inserting into a full leaf splits it in two and pushes a separator key up to the parent, which may split in turn, occasionally propagating to the root and adding a level. Deletion can merge underfull nodes, though many real implementations simply mark space free and let it be reused. This is the cost side of the bargain: every insert, update of an indexed column, and delete must maintain every index on the table, which is why indexing everything degrades write throughput and inflates storage.

Two practical consequences follow. Selectivity matters, because an index over a column with three distinct values rarely beats a scan. And covering matters: if the index contains every column the query needs, the engine can answer entirely from the leaves and skip the heap lookup altogether.
