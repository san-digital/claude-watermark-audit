1. Give yourself twelve to sixteen weeks. If you can already run five kilometres comfortably, twelve weeks is enough to build to thirteen miles safely. If you are starting from very little, take sixteen or even twenty and accept that the extra time is what protects you from injury. Rushing the build is the single most common reason people arrive at the start line already hurt.

2. Build mileage slowly. The old guidance of increasing weekly distance by no more than ten per cent is crude, but it errs in the right direction. Your heart and lungs adapt within weeks; tendons, ligaments and bone take months. When you feel suddenly capable of much more, that feeling is your cardiovascular system running ahead of your connective tissue.

3. Run most of your runs slowly. The majority of your weekly mileage should be at a conversational pace, slow enough that you could hold a phone call without gasping. Most beginners run every session at a vague medium effort, which is too hard to recover from and too easy to drive real adaptation. Going properly easy on easy days is what allows you to go properly hard on hard days.

4. Build one long run each week. This is the cornerstone session and everything else supports it. Add roughly a mile to it each week, with a shorter recovery week every third or fourth week where you cut it back by a third. Run it slower than you think you should, because its purpose is time on your feet, not pace.

5. You do not need to run thirteen miles in training. Peaking at ten to eleven miles two or three weeks before race day is plenty. Race-day adrenaline, the taper, and the crowd will comfortably carry you the extra distance. Running the full distance in training adds fatigue and injury risk without adding much fitness.

6. Include a shorter faster session once you have a base. After six or eight weeks of steady running, add one session a week with some intensity, whether that is intervals, a tempo run at a comfortably hard effort, or hill repeats. One is enough. Two hard sessions plus a long run is more than most first-timers can absorb.

7. Take rest days seriously. Adaptation happens during recovery, not during the run itself. Three or four running days a week is a perfectly good schedule for a first half marathon and leaves room for life. Resist the urge to fill rest days with something equally exhausting.

8. Get shoes that fit you, not shoes that are recommended. Go to a shop that will watch you run and let you try several pairs, and buy the ones that feel unremarkable rather than the ones with the best reviews. Replace them somewhere between five and eight hundred kilometres. Never race in shoes you have not run in.

9. Break in everything before race day. Shoes, socks, shorts, sports bra, top, belt, watch strap. Chafing that is a minor irritation at five miles becomes genuinely painful at eleven, and blood on a white shirt at mile ten is a photograph you will keep for years. Nothing new on race day is a cliché because it is true.

10. Learn to use lubricant and tape. Anti-chafe balm on the inner thighs, under the arms, around any seam, and on the feet if you are prone to blisters. Nipple tape for anyone who needs it, which is more people than admit it. Apply it before you think you need it, not once the run has started.

11. Practise your fuelling on long runs. Anything over about seventy-five minutes benefits from carbohydrate during the run, whether gels, chews, sweets or a sports drink. Your gut needs training just like your legs, so test what you plan to use, at the intervals you plan to use it, on your long runs. Discovering at mile nine that a particular gel disagrees with you is a miserable way to learn.

12. Work out your hydration in advance. Know where the water stations are on the course and decide whether you will carry your own. In cool British conditions most people finishing under two and a half hours need surprisingly little, and overdrinking is a real and occasionally dangerous mistake. Drink to thirst rather than to a schedule.

13. Eat properly the night before, not enormously. A large plate of something familiar and carbohydrate-heavy is right; a vast unfamiliar feast is not. Avoid a big jump in fibre, alcohol, or anything spicy you would not normally eat. Breakfast two to three hours before the start, again with food you have already tested.

14. Do some strength work. Two short sessions a week covering glutes, hamstrings, calves and core will do more to keep you uninjured than any amount of stretching. Single-leg work matters most, because running is a series of single-leg landings. Twenty minutes twice a week is enough to make a difference.

15. Learn the difference between soreness and injury. Muscle soreness is diffuse, symmetrical, and improves as you warm up. Injury is usually sharp, localised, on one side only, and gets worse the further you run. If something changes your gait, stop; running through an altered gait is how a small problem becomes three separate problems.

16. Take an easy week every three or four weeks. Cut your volume by around a third and keep the intensity light. It feels like losing ground and it is the opposite; the fitness you built in the previous weeks is consolidated during these lighter ones. Skipping down weeks is a reliable route to a stress fracture.

17. Practise running at your goal pace. Include some miles at your intended race pace inside your longer runs, perhaps the last three or four miles of a nine-miler. This teaches you what that effort actually feels like when you are already tired. Most first-timers have no calibrated sense of race pace and go out far too fast as a result.

18. Train on terrain that matches the course. If the race is flat, do not do all your running on hills, and if it is hilly, do not do all your running on a treadmill. Look at the elevation profile early and find somewhere locally that resembles it. Downhills in particular need practice, because they punish quadriceps that have not seen them.

19. Rehearse race morning. On one long run, get up at the time you will get up, eat what you will eat, wear what you will wear, and start at the hour the race starts. This shakes out the details you would otherwise discover on the day. If the race is early and you are not a morning runner, this rehearsal matters even more.

20. Taper for two weeks. Reduce volume by roughly a quarter in the penultimate week and by half in the final week, while keeping a small amount of race-pace running so your legs do not forget what speed feels like. You will feel sluggish, restless and convinced you are losing fitness. Everybody does; the fitness is there.

21. Plan the logistics the week before. Know how you are getting there, where you are parking, how long the bag drop queue is, and where the toilets are. Pin your number on the night before and lay everything out. Arriving stressed and rushing burns energy you will want at mile eleven.

22. Start slower than feels right. The first mile of a half marathon feels absurdly easy at the correct pace, and everyone around you will be going faster. Let them. A first mile twenty seconds too quick is repaid with interest across the final three, and negative splitting your first half marathon is one of the more satisfying things in the sport.

23. Break the race into pieces. Thirteen miles is a daunting number to hold in your head all at once. Think of it as four miles, then four more, then a 5K with a bit on the end, or count down to the next water station. The middle section, roughly miles seven to ten, is where the mental work happens.

24. Expect a bad patch and plan for it. Almost everyone hits a stretch where the pace feels unsustainable and quitting seems reasonable. It usually passes within a mile or two if you shorten your stride slightly, relax your shoulders, and take some fuel. Knowing in advance that it is coming removes most of its power.

25. Recover properly afterwards. Walk rather than immediately sitting down, eat and drink something within the hour, and take at least a few days completely off running. Give yourself a fortnight of very easy running before any hard sessions, and do not sign up for the next thing while still high on the finish line. The people who keep running for decades are the ones who respect the week after the race as much as the week before it.
