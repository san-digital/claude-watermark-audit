Proposals for a canal across Central America circulated for centuries, but construction only began in 1881, when a French company led by Ferdinand de Lesseps started work in Panama. De Lesseps had built the Suez Canal at sea level and insisted the same approach would serve here. It would not. The isthmus is mountainous, geologically unstable and drenched by rain, and the Chagres River flooded violently across the intended route. Disease was worse still: mosquito-borne yellow fever and malaria killed workers faster than they could be recruited, and by the time the company failed in 1889 something in the order of twenty thousand people had died.

The United States acquired the concession and resumed work in 1904, after supporting Panama's separation from Colombia. Its engineers made the decision the French had resisted. Rather than cutting a channel down to sea level, they would raise ships over the continental divide. Damming the Chagres at Gatun produced a vast artificial lake roughly twenty-six metres above the oceans, which simultaneously controlled the river and provided a navigable stretch across most of the crossing. Chief engineer John Frank Stevens established the plan and the railway logistics; George Washington Goethals completed it. Colonel William Gorgas attacked the mosquito breeding grounds with drainage, fumigation and screening, and the death rate fell sharply. The first transit took place on 15 August 1914.

Three flights of locks do the lifting. Gatun raises ships in three steps from the Caribbean to the lake. On the Pacific side Pedro Miguel lowers them one step into the Miraflores Lake, and Miraflores lowers them two further steps to the sea. Each original chamber is 33.5 metres wide and around 320 metres long, and those dimensions dictated the Panamax standard that governed merchant ship design for decades.

The mechanism is gravity throughout. There are no pumps. Enormous culverts, roughly five and a half metres across, run inside the lock walls and the centre wall, with branch culverts leading to a grid of openings in the floor of each chamber. Opening the valves lets water from the level above flow down and rise evenly across the whole chamber floor, so a ship lifts smoothly rather than being pushed about by a jet. A chamber fills or empties in eight to ten minutes, and a full transit takes eight to ten hours.

Vessels do not tie up to the walls. Electric locomotives called mules run on toothed rails along the lock sides, holding the ship centred while it moves under its own power. The gates are hollow steel miter gates that float in water, hinged so that they meet at an angle pointing towards the higher level, which means water pressure seals them tighter.

The whole system runs on rainfall. Every lockage releases fresh water from Gatun Lake to the sea, so drought directly reduces the number of ships that can cross. The expansion opened in 2016 added a third lane with larger chambers, rolling gates and side basins that recycle much of the water used.
