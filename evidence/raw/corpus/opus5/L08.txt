The three cuisines of the western end of Europe share a great deal, and each insists on its differences. France codified; Spain regionalised and refused to be codified; Portugal looked outward across the Atlantic and brought the results home. All three rest on the same essential trio of wheat, the vine and the olive, and all three diverge the moment you move from one province to the next.

French cooking owes its international grammar to two men. Antonin Carême organised the sauces of the early nineteenth century into families, and Auguste Escoffier, working from the kitchens of the Savoy and the Carlton, reduced that inheritance to a workable professional system in Le Guide culinaire of 1903. The mother sauces he described, béchamel, velouté, espagnole, hollandaise and tomate, are still taught, and the brigade de cuisine he imposed on the kitchen, with its chef de partie, saucier, poissonnier, garde-manger and pâtissier, still governs how a serious restaurant is staffed. What Escoffier did not do was invent the food. That came from the regions, and the regions have never surrendered.

In the Périgord and the Dordogne the cooking runs on fat and on fungus. Confit de canard, duck legs cured in salt and then poached slowly in their own rendered fat, keeps through the winter in a stone jar. Foie gras, whatever one thinks of its production, is treated there as a staple rather than a luxury, served as a terrine or seared and set against something acidic. The black truffle, Tuber melanosporum, comes from the same limestone country, and a sauce Périgueux is simply a Madeira-based sauce carrying chopped truffle. Cèpes, gathered in autumn oak woods, are sautéed with garlic and persillade and very little else.

Go south-east and the fat changes from duck to olive oil. Provençal cooking is built on tomato, garlic, anchovy and herbs: tapenade, aïoli thick enough to stand a spoon in, ratatouille, pissaladière from Nice with its onions and olives, socca made of chickpea flour and cooked on a wide copper pan over wood. Bouillabaisse, the Marseillais fish stew, is properly a fisherman's dish of what would not sell, saffron and fennel and rascasse, served with rouille and croûtons. Further west, Languedoc and Gascony argue over cassoulet, the great slow gratin of white beans with confit, sausage and pork, with Toulouse, Castelnaudary and Carcassonne each claiming the authentic version.

The north-east cooks differently again. Alsace, German in language and French in citizenship depending on the century, produces choucroute garnie, tarte flambée and Riesling that tastes nothing like a Rhine wine. Burgundy gives bœuf bourguignon, coq au vin, œufs en meurette and jambon persillé, all leaning on the red wines of the Côte d'Or. Normandy substitutes butter, cream and cider for oil and wine, and offers Camembert and Livarot; Brittany answers with salted butter, galettes de sarrasin and crêpes. Lyon, which many French cooks regard as the real capital of the table, has its bouchons serving quenelles de brochet, andouillette, salade lyonnaise and gratin dauphinois, none of it refined and all of it excellent. The cheeses alone, Comté, Roquefort, Époisses, Reblochon, Sainte-Maure de Touraine, Beaufort, would sustain a separate literature, and the pâtisserie tradition of mille-feuille, tarte Tatin, éclair, madeleine and macaron is essentially an engineering discipline.

Spain resists this kind of systematisation, partly because it is not really one culinary country. In Andalucía the Moorish inheritance is still legible in almonds, saffron, cumin and aubergine. The heat produces cold soups: gazpacho, and the thicker, richer salmorejo of Córdoba, made with bread, tomato, oil and garlic and finished with jamón and chopped egg. Pescaíto frito, small fish dusted in flour and fried in very hot oil, is a Cádiz speciality, and the fortified wines of Jerez de la Frontera, from the bone-dry fino and manzanilla to oloroso and Pedro Ximénez, come from the same triangle of chalky albariza soil.

Jamón ibérico is a national argument in itself. The best of it, jamón ibérico de bellota, comes from black-hoofed pigs fattened on acorns in the oak pasture called dehesa in Extremadura and around Jabugo in Huelva, then cured for three or four years. It is sliced by hand, thin enough to be translucent, and is never cooked.

Valencia claims paella and is severe about it. The Valencian original contains rabbit, chicken, garrofó beans, judía verde, saffron and sometimes snails, and specifically does not contain chorizo, a point on which the region will not negotiate. Rice cooked in a wide shallow pan is a whole family of dishes there, including arroz a banda, arròs negre coloured with squid ink, and fideuà made with short noodles instead of rice.

The Basque Country, País Vasco, has arguably the densest concentration of serious cooking in Europe. San Sebastián bars serve pintxos on the counter, and the kitchens behind them produce bacalao al pil-pil, in which salt cod, garlic and olive oil are emulsified by nothing more than the gelatine in the fish and a patient circular motion of the pan. Txakoli, poured from a height, cuts through it. The nueva cocina vasca movement of the 1970s, and later Ferran Adrià at elBulli in Cataluña, made Spain the most experimentally ambitious cooking country in the world for about two decades.

Cataluña itself contributes escalivada, pa amb tomàquet, calçots eaten with romesco in a spring ritual that requires a bib, and crema catalana, the older cousin of crème brûlée. Galicia, wet and Atlantic, offers pulpo a la gallega dressed with pimentón and coarse salt, empanada, percebes, and the crisp albariño of the Rías Baixas. Asturias drinks cider poured from arm's length and eats fabada. Castilla contributes cocido, lechazo roasted in a wood oven, and the saffron of La Mancha alongside its queso manchego.

Portugal is smaller and, in its own way, more consistent. Bacalhau, salt cod, is the national obsession, brought from the Newfoundland fisheries and said to have a different preparation for every day of the year: bacalhau à Brás with shredded potato and egg, bacalhau com natas, pastéis de bacalhau, or simply grilled with boiled potatoes and a great deal of olive oil. Caldo verde, from the Minho, is potato broth with finely shredded couve-galega and a slice of chouriço. Porto claims the francesinha, a sandwich armoured in cheese and beer sauce that is not elegant and does not care.

The Algarve cooks in a cataplana, a hinged copper clam of a pan, producing shellfish steamed with pork, coriander and garlic; carne de porco à alentejana marries pork with clams in the same spirit. The Alentejo also gives açorda, bread soup with coriander, garlic and a poached egg, which is peasant cooking of the most literal kind. Leitão à Bairrada, suckling pig roasted with garlic and pepper, is a pilgrimage dish. Sardinhas assadas on charcoal in June, for the festivals of Santo António and São João, perfume entire cities.

Portuguese pastry deserves its own paragraph, and its origins are conventual: convents used egg whites to starch habits and clarify wine, leaving surplus yolks that the nuns turned into an entire genre of doces conventuais. The pastel de nata, whose recipe descends from the Mosteiro dos Jerónimos in Belém, is the famous example, but ovos moles from Aveiro, pão de ló, toucinho do céu and bolo-rei belong to the same tradition. To drink there is vinho verde from the north, the great fortified wines of the Douro and Madeira, and ginjinha in a small glass on the way home.

What links all three is an insistence that a dish belongs somewhere. Ask in Marseille, in València or in Aveiro how a thing should be made, and the answer will begin with a place before it mentions an ingredient.
