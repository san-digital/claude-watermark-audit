## Headline limitation — read this before anything else

**Nothing in this report can prove or disprove that Anthropic embeds a
proprietary statistical watermark in Claude's output.** Every test below
operates on the *visible surface* of generated text — words, characters,
punctuation, sentence lengths. A real token-level statistical watermark
(the kind described in the watermarking literature, e.g. green/red
vocabulary partitioning keyed by a hash of preceding tokens) operates on
the model's **token probability distribution before sampling**, which this
environment cannot see:

- No token log-probabilities are exposed here. **Test not possible in this
  environment.**
- No control over temperature, top-p, top-k or seed is available through
  the Agent tool. **Test not possible in this environment.**
- No access to Anthropic's detector, key, or published method exists here.

Consequently, any regularity found below — a repeated word, a punctuation
rate, a positional pattern — is **equally consistent with ordinary model
style** as with deliberate marking. This report finds no structure that
requires a watermarking explanation, and several instances where an
apparently model-specific pattern turns out to be present in un-marked
19th-century human prose or genre-driven, at rates that fully explain it
by chance. But absence of detected structure is not proof of absence of a
watermark either — a scheme carried purely in token selection would leave
**no** trace in these metrics and nothing here could detect it. Both
failure modes (over-claiming and under-claiming) are treated as equally
serious throughout.

## Methods

Three new scripts were written for this analysis, stdlib-only (no
numpy/scipy/pandas were available or used):

- `scripts/stat_common.py` — shared utilities: word/sentence proxy
  tokenizers, punctuation taxonomy, Wilson score intervals, percentile
  bootstrap (resampling via `random`), permutation tests for
  autocorrelation and for difference-in-means, a Monte Carlo
  goodness-of-fit test for modular periodicity, a two-proportion z-test,
  Shannon entropy, and a `TestLedger` that records every p-value produced
  anywhere in the analysis for a global correction.
- `scripts/stat_lexical_punct.py` — word frequency, character/word
  n-grams (n=1..4), punctuation rates by model and category, sentence
  length distributions, a curated "LLM-ism" lexical marker list,
  cross-model vs. within-model spread, and generic corpus metrics
  (entropy, mean word length, type-token ratio) for model vs. human vs.
  synthetic.
- `scripts/stat_positional.py` — the repeat-generation descriptive test
  (with the provenance caveat applied explicitly), autocorrelation of
  sentence-length and word-length sequences with permutation p-values, and
  Monte Carlo chi-square tests for periodic bias in the spacing between
  punctuation marks — run identically on the model corpora, the human
  control, and the synthetic controls, so the controls calibrate the
  test's own false-positive behaviour on text known not to be
  Claude-generated.

Both scripts were executed for real; every number in this report is copied
from their actual JSON output, not estimated. No proprietary tokenizer
exists here, so **word-proxy tokens (Unicode letter runs) and character
n-grams stand in for true subword tokens throughout** — this is stated
once here and applies to every "word" and "token" reference below.

### Multiple comparisons

This analysis ran a large number of hypothesis tests. Every p-value —
punctuation-vs-human z-tests, lexical-marker-vs-human z-tests,
sentence-length permutation tests, sentence/word-length autocorrelation
permutation tests, and punctuation-gap periodicity Monte Carlo tests — was
logged to a `TestLedger`. Totals:

| Script | Tests run | Nominal α=0.05 hits | Expected by chance (n×0.05) | Bonferroni α | Bonferroni-surviving hits |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `stat_lexical_punct.py` | 180 | 40 | 9.0 | 2.78×10⁻⁴ | 29 |
| `stat_positional.py` | 525 | 113 | 26.25 | 9.52×10⁻⁵ | **0** |
| **Combined** | **705** | **153** | **35.25** | **7.09×10⁻⁵** | **29** |

Two things follow directly from this table, and both matter for reading
everything below:

- In the positional/periodicity battery (525 tests), **zero** results
  survive Bonferroni correction — including in the human control, which
  was tested with exactly the same procedure. This is the honest headline
  result of the positional analysis: nothing found there is distinguishable
  from chance once the number of tests is accounted for.
- In the lexical/punctuation battery, 29 of 180 tests survive Bonferroni.
  All 29 are **model-vs-human** comparisons (punctuation typography and a
  handful of lexical markers), not model-vs-model comparisons — i.e. they
  say models write differently from 19th-century Gutenberg prose, which is
  expected and uninteresting on its own; genre and register, not marking,
  are the obvious explanation and are discussed under Confounders.

Bonferroni is deliberately conservative here (it treats all 705 tests as
independent, which they are not — many are correlated, e.g. testing the
same punctuation mark across four models against the same human baseline).
Where a specific finding is discussed below, its uncorrected p-value,
whether it survives Bonferroni, and the number of related tests it sits
among are all given so the reader can judge for themselves rather than
trust a single threshold.

## Results

### 1. Word and token-proxy frequency distributions

Top words are dominated by function words in every corpus, as expected of
English prose (`the`, `and`, `a`, `of`, `in`, `to` in the top 6 for all
four models). Sample: opus5 top three — `the` (1,216, 62.1/1,000 words),
`and` (687, 35.1/1,000), `a` (648, 33.1/1,000), out of 19,585 word-proxy
tokens. haiku45's top-10 list is the only one to surface a non-function
word this high (`self`, rank 9, 105 occurrences) — this is source-code
identifier leakage (Python `self.` in class methods) into the aggregate
word count, not a linguistic anomaly; it reflects that haiku45's `list`
tokens diffuse into fewer, more repetitive code identifiers relative to
its other categories at this corpus size. Full top-25 lists per model are
in the script's JSON output.

**Type-token ratio (TTR)**, size-matched at N=15,781 words (the smallest
model corpus) for fair comparison: opus5 0.250, sonnet5 0.248, haiku45
0.243, fable5 0.268, human control 0.124. All four models show roughly
double the human control's TTR at the same sample size — expected, since
the model corpus mixes ten distinct topics/genres per model while the
human control is continuous narrative reusing the same character names and
vocabulary; this is a **genre/composition effect**, not evidence of
anything about marking.

*Classification: `Observed anomaly, not established as watermark` (word
frequency shape is ordinary English function-word distribution; TTR gap
vs. human is fully explained by corpus composition).*

### 2. Repeated lexical/synonym choices (n-grams) across models

Character 3-grams and word 2/3-grams with the largest "excess rate vs. the
other three models" were computed per model. On inspection, **the
overwhelming majority of "distinctive" n-grams are prompt-content**, not
style: e.g. all four models' top word 3-grams for the L05 tokenizer/parser
prompt include variants of `self current token`, `assert calculate
assert`/`assert evaluate assert` (from the required inline assertions),
and all four models' L06 JSON prompt outputs surface `crew notes`/`crew
notes the` (from the required `notes` field about aircraft crew). This is
the identical-prompts confound working as expected — four models
answering the same code/JSON prompt necessarily reuse the same
identifiers and required field names. After discounting prompt-driven
overlap, the remaining distinctive items are unremarkable stylistic
tics (opus5 and fable5 both show elevated `---` as a 3-gram, i.e. more
use of markdown-style horizontal rules/section breaks in list-format
answers than the other two models; sonnet5 shows elevated `":"` /`","`
3-grams, consistent with slightly more JSON-adjacent punctuation density).

*Classification: `Not supported by evidence` (as a marking signal) —
n-gram distinctiveness here is explained by shared prompt content and
ordinary formatting preference, both identified directly from the data.*

### 6. Repeated generations from an identical prompt (S01/S10, M01/M10, L01/L10)

**Read the constraint first: this test cannot measure sampling
determinism, temperature effects, or watermark-driven repeatability.**
All 30 samples per model were generated in one conversation, in prompt
order, so S10 follows S01 in the same context and a model can simply
recall/re-derive its earlier answer rather than re-sample. The result
below is reported purely as a descriptive same/different-text fact, as
instructed:

| Model | S01 vs S10 | M01 vs M10 | L01 vs L10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| opus5 | not identical (difflib ratio 0.43, word Jaccard 0.24) | not identical (0.50, 0.39) | not identical (0.53, 0.43) |
| sonnet5 | not identical (0.52, 0.41) | not identical (0.40, 0.34) | not identical (0.32, 0.32) |
| haiku45 | **byte-identical** (ratio 1.00) | **byte-identical** | **byte-identical** |
| fable5 | not identical (0.46, 0.28) | not identical (0.45, 0.37) | not identical (0.40, 0.32) |

haiku45's byte-identical repeats are the one large qualitative difference
across models here, and the provenance notes already give the correct,
non-mysterious explanation: haiku45 (fully) reproduced its earlier answer
from context, while the other three models varied their answer to some
degree despite having equal in-context access to their own earlier
response. That variation-vs-repetition split between models is itself
mildly interesting as a behavioural note, but it says nothing about
sampling determinism or watermarking, because the confound (in-context
availability of the earlier answer) applies identically to all four
models and this design cannot separate "chose to repeat itself" from
"was forced to by low temperature."

*Classification: `Test not possible in this environment` (as a
determinism/watermark test, per the stated constraint) — the raw
same/different fact itself is `Confirmed by reproduced experiment`, but
is not diagnostic of anything beyond the confound.*

### 7. Cross-model comparison on identical prompts vs. within-model variation

For each of the 27 non-repeat prompts, word-proxy count was compared
across the 4 models (cross-model spread) against the spread of word
counts across the 9 different prompts of the same length band within one
model (within-model spread):

| Band | Mean within-model SD (word count) across 4 models | Example cross-model SD on one prompt |
|---|---:|---:|
| short (target 50–100 words) | 14.4–25.2 | S01: SD 2.9 (mean 100.0, range 96–104) |
| medium (target 300–500 words) | 28.4–47.9 | M01: SD 65.9 (mean 415.2, range 337–484); M03: SD 37.7 (mean 459.0) |
| long (target ≥1000 words) | 104.1–198.8 | L01: SD 213.5 (mean 1282.2, range 1116–1649); L03: SD 204.6 |

For the `short` band, models track each other very tightly on the same
prompt (SD ≈ 3 words) — far tighter than any one model varies across its
own 9 different short prompts (SD 14–25 words). This makes sense: short
prompts specify an exact 50–100 word target, and all four models hit it
similarly well; cross-model agreement here reflects instruction-following
consensus, not shared internal state.

For `medium` and `long` bands, cross-model spread (SD 38–214, depending on
prompt) is of the **same order of magnitude as, and sometimes larger
than**, within-model spread (SD 41–199) — i.e. on longer, less
tightly-specified prompts, models differ from each other by about as much
as a single model differs across its own different prompts. This is
consistent with ordinary model-to-model stylistic variation in verbosity
(opus5 in particular tends toward the high end on `long` factual/technical
prompts — 1,649 words on L01, 1,645 on L03, both notably above the other
three models), not with any shared/marked structure.

*Classification: `Observed anomaly, not established as watermark`
(quantified, real verbosity differences between models; ordinary
stylistic variation is a sufficient explanation).*

### 8. Positional structure (periodicity test)

This is the test most directly relevant to "could a payload be encoded
positionally," and it was run identically on all four model corpora, the
human control, and a synthetic random-ASCII control with **no linguistic
structure at all**, specifically so the controls calibrate the test's own
false-positive rate on text that is known not to carry any payload.

**Method:** (a) autocorrelation (Pearson r) of the sentence-length
sequence and the word-length sequence at lags 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, tested
against a shuffle-based permutation null (500/200 permutations depending
on sequence length); (b) for punctuation markers (comma, period, all
terminal punctuation), the gaps between successive marker positions were
tested for periodic bias at candidate periods k ∈ {2,3,4,5,6,7,8,10,12}
via a Monte Carlo chi-square goodness-of-fit test against the null "gap
mod k is uniform," 150–300 replicates depending on sample size. This is a
fixed, generic, pre-registered set of lags/periods — not a search for any
specific claimed watermark key, consistent with the hard limit against
attempting to reconstruct a watermarking scheme. Very long control texts
(the human corpus) were capped (4,000 sentences / 30,000 words for
autocorrelation, 12,000 gap samples for the periodicity test, both applied
identically wherever used) purely for tractability in pure-Python; this is
recorded as a limitation, not hidden.

**Headline result: after Bonferroni correction for the 525 tests in this
battery, zero results survive in any corpus, including the human
control.** This is the cleanest, most decisive finding in this report.

Two calibration facts make the "why" clear:

- **Sentence-length autocorrelation "hits" are ubiquitous in ordinary
  prose, not model-specific.** All 5 human-control files showed nominally
  significant (p<0.05, uncorrected) lag-1 through lag-8 sentence-length
  autocorrelation (25 of 25 file×lag tests run — one file per lag, capped
  at 4,000 sentences each), and the human control also showed all 5
  word-length-autocorrelation lags significant. The per-model hit rate was
  much lower and patchier out of a larger number of file×lag combinations
  per model (more prose files per model than the human control's 5 books):
  opus5 8 of 72, sonnet5 4 of 68, haiku45 3 of 75, fable5 7 of 70. In other
  words, the human control — indisputably not Claude output — shows a much
  *higher proportion* of "significant" file×lag tests (100%) than any
  model does (4-11%). Sentence-length clustering (writers keep a rhythm;
  short sentences follow short sentences) is a well-known, mundane
  property of written prose. It is not periodic in the watermark sense (no
  fixed spacing recoverable — see below) and every corpus, model and
  human alike, shows it to some degree, most strongly in the human
  control.
- **Punctuation-gap "periodicity" hits are also present in the human
  control and, at chance rate, in pure noise.** The human control hit
  16 of 27 candidate (marker × k) combinations at nominal p<0.05 (vs. an
  expected ~1.35 by chance) — actually more hits than any single model
  (opus5 7/27, sonnet5 7/27, haiku45 4/27, fable5 10/27). The synthetic
  random-ASCII control (genuinely unstructured, seeded-random text) hit
  2 of 27 — close to the ~1.35 expected by chance alone, exactly as it
  should for a well-calibrated test on real noise. The gap between "human
  prose: 16/27 hits" and "random ASCII: 2/27 hits" shows this specific
  test's null model (gap-mod-k uniform under i.i.d. sampling) does not
  perfectly fit ordinary language — natural gap-length distributions
  between punctuation marks are not flat, which biases some residues for
  small k regardless of any real periodicity. k=7 came up "significant"
  for the period/terminal-punctuation marker in **all four models**, which
  looks like a striking cross-model coincidence in isolation — but the
  human control *also* hits k=7 for both comma and period, and hits more
  k-values overall than any model does. A test that flags Jane Austen and
  Herman Melville as more "periodic" than three of the four Claude models
  cannot be used as evidence that the Claude models are marked; it is
  evidence that the test's assumed null doesn't hold for natural-language
  punctuation spacing in general, which is a limitation of the test, not a
  finding about Claude.

**What would have to be true for a genuine positional/periodic watermark
to be recoverable by this kind of test, and why it isn't shown here:** a
real positional payload would need a period or lag whose "hit" (a) is
present with an effect size well above what human/synthetic-noise controls
produce under the identical procedure, (b) survives correction for the
number of periods/lags tested, and (c) is stable across independent
files/prompts within a model rather than appearing in a handful of files
and not others. None of the 525 tests here meet even the first of these
three bars once controls are accounted for. This absence is meaningful
evidence *against* a positional-surface-feature payload of the kind
tested — but it says nothing about a token-selection-level watermark,
which would leave no positional footprint in sentence or word lengths at
all (see Headline limitation).

*Classification: `Not supported by evidence` (positional/periodic
structure in surface features, as tested) for both the sentence/word-length
autocorrelation and the punctuation-gap periodicity tests.*

Bonferroni). haiku45's most frequent hits are `crucial` (5), `navigate`
(5), `landscape`/`robust`/`comprehensive`/`journey` (2 each) — small raw
counts (23 total hits in 15,781 words) that produce a real but modest
effect; this should not be over-read given how few of the 36 terms drive
it. It is a genuine, quantified stylistic property of this haiku45
sample, not proof of anything about marking, and it inherits ordinary
sampling noise at this count (n=23).

(haiku45's elevated "LLM-ism" word rate; entropy/word-length findings are
`Not supported by evidence` as anything beyond ordinary composition
effects).*

## What would have to be true for a statistical watermark to be
## detectable here, and why it isn't

A detectable statistical (token-level) watermark of the kind in the public
literature requires, at minimum:
1. Access to per-token probabilities or the sampled token sequence itself
   (not just the rendered text) — **unavailable**: no tokenizer, no
   logits, no API access in this environment.
2. Control of, or at least visibility into, sampling parameters
   (temperature, seed) to distinguish marked bias from ordinary sampling
   noise — **unavailable**.
3. Either the detector/key (to test directly) or a very large, controlled
   sample under identical conditions to detect a small distributional
   bias statistically — **the corpus here is small (16k–20k words per
   model) and was not generated under controlled sampling conditions**
   (single context, prompt order, per the provenance notes).
4. A well-calibrated null model for whatever surface statistic is used —
   this analysis found, concretely, that its own periodicity test's null
   assumption does not perfectly hold even for the human control, which
   is exactly the kind of false-positive risk that makes surface-feature
   detection unreliable without (1) and (2).

None of these four are available in this environment. The positional and
distributional tests run here are the closest approximation possible
without them, and they returned no structure that survives correction or
that isn't equally present in un-marked human text. That is the strongest
statement this analysis can honestly make.

## Confounders identified

- **Genre.** The human control is dialogue-dense 19th-century fiction; the
  model corpus mixes factual prose, informal social writing, technical
  explanation, lists, code, JSON, and narrative dialogue. Every
  model-vs-human comparison in this report either uses the
  genre-matched `quoted_speech` subset (S09/M09/L09) explicitly, or states
  plainly that genre is uncontrolled (e.g. the full-corpus punctuation and
  entropy tables).
- **Corpus generation order/context.** All 30 samples per model share one
  conversation context in prompt order — invalidates the repeat-prompt
  test as a determinism probe (see item 6) and means later-prompt outputs
  are not fully independent draws even outside the repeat pairs.
- **sonnet5's split generation context** for L01–L05 vs. the rest of its
  corpus — a mild confound specifically for sonnet5's `long`-band
  distributional numbers (sentence length, punctuation rate in that band).
- **Prompt-content overlap driving apparent n-gram "distinctiveness."**
  Identical prompts across models mean shared required vocabulary (code
  identifiers, JSON field names) will always look like a "distinctive
  n-gram" unless explicitly checked against prompt content, as done here.
- **Sample size asymmetry.** Model corpora (~16k–20k words each) are two
  orders of magnitude smaller than the human control (540k words); every
  comparison used rates + intervals for this reason, but small model-side
  counts (e.g. 5–23 hits for individual lexical markers) still carry wide
  uncertainty that is reported, not hidden.
- **Word/sentence tokenization is a heuristic proxy**, not a linguistic
  parser or a true model tokenizer. It will over/under-split on
  abbreviations, decimals, and code; this is why sentence-length analysis
  was restricted to prose categories only.

## Confidence statement

This analysis is built entirely on reproducible, stdlib-only Python run
against the frozen corpus and controls, with every rate reported alongside
its sample size and an uncertainty interval (Wilson score intervals for
proportions/rates, percentile bootstrap or permutation p-values for
differences and autocorrelations), and with a full accounting of the 705
hypothesis tests run and how many "significant" results would be expected
by chance alone. Where a finding could plausibly be read as evidence of
model-specific marking, this report explicitly tested that reading against
a human control and, for the positional battery, a synthetic-noise control
run through the identical procedure — and in the one place this mattered
most (positional/periodic structure), the human control showed *more* of
the pattern than the Claude models did, which is strong evidence against
reading it as marking.

The confidence level in every "no watermark signal found here" statement
is bounded by the Headline Limitation above: this is a surface-statistics
audit with no access to tokens, logits, sampling control, or Anthropic's
detector, on a corpus of ~16k-20k words per model. It can rule out
crude, high-effect-size surface encodings; it cannot rule out, and does
not claim to rule out, a token-level statistical watermark of the kind
described in the academic literature, which would leave no trace visible
to any test performed here.
